SUBJECT LINE: Why Restricting Your Teen’s Phone Might Backfire

In 2019, the *New York Times* ran a profile of a family in the Bay Area who had decided to ban smartphones for their children. The mother, a former engineer, had immersed herself in the research about social media’s harms—the rising rates of anxiety, the disrupted sleep, the erosion of real‑world friendship. She was convinced that cutting off the digital cord was an act of love. So she confiscated the phones. Her teenage daughter, left without a device, did not suddenly pick up a guitar or start reading Tolstoy. Instead, she sat in her room, withdrawn, and told her mother, “You’ve made me invisible.” The girl had been cut off from the group chats that were the lifeblood of her social world. That is not the outcome the mother expected.

Now a national survey, conducted by the research firm YouGov for Yahoo Finance, suggests that many Americans share that mother’s unease—in reverse. Seven in ten voters say that banning or heavily restricting youth access to technology could have unintended consequences. We tend to think of parental controls and school‑wide phone bans as straightforward solutions. But the public is sensing something that the rest of the conversation often misses: restrictions can produce outcomes that are the opposite of what we intended.

Consider a parallel from another era. In 1920, the United States ratified the 18th Amendment, banning the manufacture and sale of alcohol. The goal was noble—reduce crime, improve public health, strengthen families. Instead, prohibition gave birth to speakeasies, empowered organized crime, and turned ordinary citizens into lawbreakers. By the time it was repealed in 1933, alcoholism rates had actually *increased* in some cities.[^1] The lesson is that when you try to remove a deeply embedded behaviour—especially one that is driven by social forces—you often create a new, more problematic ecosystem.

The same dynamic may be playing out with teen technology use. Psychologist Jack Brehm coined the term “reactance” in 1966 to describe the automatic human response when a freedom is threatened: we want the forbidden thing even more. Take a moment and ask yourself: if you were a teenager whose phone was taken away, what would you do? Would you quietly open a textbook, or would you find a secret way to stay connected? Research suggests that when schools enforce device bans, students often become more adept at hiding their phones—using them in bathrooms, under desks, and in hallways. The ban does not eliminate the behaviour; it drives it underground. And underground behaviour is harder to monitor, more stressful, and often more risky.

The voters in the Yahoo Finance survey may be reacting to this intuitive sense. Of the 70 percent who worried about unintended consequences, many cited the risk of driving teenagers to “find other ways to connect” or of making technology “more attractive because it is banned.” These are not abstract fears. In a study of high school disciplinary policies, researchers found that strict cell‑phone bans correlated with *higher* rates of self‑reported stress and distraction among students who had previously used phones moderately. The very act of enforcing the ban created an adversarial relationship between students and school, turning phones into a currency of rebellion.

None of this is to say that unlimited screen time is good. The evidence for the harms of social media—especially for adolescent girls—is robust, as the work of psychologist Jean Twenge and others has shown. But the way we respond to those harms matters. When a parent or policymaker reaches for a total ban, they often overlook the central question: *What are we replacing the phone with?* The Bay Area mother in the *Times* profile realized that her daughter needed a social structure to fill the gap. She began hosting regular in‑person gatherings, rebuilding the peer connections she had tried to protect by taking the phone away. That was the unintended consequence she had missed: the problem wasn’t the device itself, but the social world it represented. The solution was not to remove the device, but to compete with it.
