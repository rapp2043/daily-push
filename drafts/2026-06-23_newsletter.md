SUBJECT LINE: The Burnout Paradox: Why AI Might Be Making Doctors More Exhausted

In his book *Deep Medicine*, Dr. Eric Topol, the cardiologist and digital-health researcher, painted a future in which artificial intelligence would rescue physicians from the soul-crushing grind of electronic health records. Machines, he argued, could handle the pattern recognition, the data entry, the mountains of imaging. And then doctors would be free to do what they were trained to do: listen, examine, connect. It was a beautiful vision. But here’s the problem with beautiful visions. They don’t always survive contact with reality.

Two recent articles published in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research* set out to test that very assumption. One examined how AI clinical decision-support tools changed the daily experience of frontline clinicians. The other looked at the relationship between AI adoption and burnout rates. The researchers expected to find that more AI meant less burnout. Instead, they found something far more interesting—and far more unsettling.

Let’s start with the first study. It tracked a group of physicians in a large health system as they began using an AI tool designed to flag potential misdiagnoses in the emergency department. The tool was smart. It caught things the doctors missed. But the doctors did not feel grateful. They felt anxious. Every time the AI popped up with a suggestion, they had to stop, evaluate it, and decide whether to override it. That constant second-guessing turned out to be cognitively exhausting. One physician described it as having a backseat driver who was sometimes right and sometimes wrong—but always talking. The study concluded that the tool improved diagnostic accuracy, but it also increased the perceived workload. The net effect on burnout? Neutral, at best.

Now for the second study. This one was broader: a survey of nearly a thousand healthcare workers across multiple specialties, asking about their experiences with AI systems. What the researchers found was a striking split. The workers who described AI as a “collaborator” reported lower burnout. Those who described AI as a “supervisor” reported dramatically higher burnout. It turned out that the *way* the technology was implemented mattered more than the technology itself. When AI was presented as a tool to be consulted, doctors felt empowered. When it was presented as a system that monitored their performance, they felt surveilled. And surveillance, as we know, is a fast track to exhaustion.

But there’s a deeper pattern here, one that extends well beyond medicine. We like to think that automation removes friction. That it takes the drudgery out of work. But what these studies remind us is that friction is not always bad. Sometimes friction is feedback. It forces you to engage. And removing *all* friction—handing the decision over to an algorithm—can leave the human operator feeling both bored and anxious, a strange hybrid of underload and overload. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called this state the opposite of flow. He called it psychic entropy.

Here’s a thought experiment. Imagine you’re a radiologist. You’ve been reading mammograms for twenty years. You have a finely tuned intuition. One day, a new AI tool starts highlighting suspicious areas on every image. Sometimes it catches things you would have missed. But sometimes it flags perfectly normal tissue. How do you respond? You can’t ignore it. You can’t fully trust it. So you do what any sensible professional does: you check everything twice. That slows you down. That erodes your confidence in your own judgment. And over time, that wears you out. The tool that was supposed to make your life easier has made it more complicated.

This is the counterintuitive truth that the JMIR studies point to. AI doesn’t simply replace human judgment; it reconfigures it. And that reconfiguration has a hidden cost.
