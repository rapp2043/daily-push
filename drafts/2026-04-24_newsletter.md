SUBJECT LINE: Why the Smartest Students Are Being Taught the Wrong Thing

Dr. Baruch Fischhoff, a psychologist at Carnegie Mellon University, once asked a group of high school seniors to think about something they had considered doing that was risky. He asked them to list all the possible good things that could come from it, and then all the possible bad things. The teenagers could do this as thoroughly as any adult. They knew the risks. They knew the consequences. But then Fischhoff gave them a second task. He asked them to rate how likely each outcome was—on a scale from zero to a hundred. That’s where the trouble started. The teenagers, it turned out, were terrible at probabilities. They dramatically overestimated the chance of something exciting happening and underestimated the chance of a bad outcome, even when they had just listed it themselves. They weren’t ignoring the facts. They were misjudging the odds.

Now think about what happens in a typical American high school. Students spend thousands of hours learning the steps of the Krebs cycle, the dates of the Civil War, the rules of grammar. They absorb a vast catalog of facts and procedures. And then, one day, they are handed a life-altering decision—which college to attend, whether to take on debt, which career path to pursue—and they have almost zero formal training in how to make that choice. We teach them *what* to think, but almost never *how* to think about decisions.

This is the argument at the heart of a growing movement called Decision Education. And it is not a fringe idea. The Alliance for Decision Education, a nonprofit backed by researchers from Wharton and MIT, has been working to bring a curriculum of decision-making into K-12 schools. Their premise is simple: the ability to make good decisions is a more reliable predictor of life outcomes than grades or test scores. But right now, it is almost entirely absent from the classroom. According to a recent article in *eSchool News*, career readiness programs focus on technical and soft skills—coding, teamwork, communication—while the foundational skill of sound decision-making goes largely undressed.

Let me offer three specific abilities that decision education targets, because they reveal just how much we’ve been missing.

The first is *framing*. Before you can evaluate a decision, you have to know what kind of decision it is. Is it one you can reverse easily, or is it high-stakes and irreversible? Is it a choice between two options, or do you first need to create better options? Research shows that most people—teenagers especially—jump straight into weighing pros and cons without asking, “Am I even looking at the right set of choices?” A simple act like generating a third alternative can transform a bad decision. In one study published in the *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*, participants who were prompted to “imagine you could change the options” ended up making choices that led to significantly higher satisfaction months later.

The second skill is *probability calibration*. Humans are notoriously bad at estimating likelihoods. We are seduced by vivid stories and misled by recent events. Fischhoff’s own work on “hindsight bias” showed that once we know an outcome, we believe we knew it all along—which makes it hard to learn from mistakes. Decision education teaches students to assign numeric probabilities to their beliefs and then track how often they are right. Over time, they become what psychologists call “well-calibrated.” A 2019 study led by researcher Ellen Peters at the University of Oregon found that even a short intervention—teaching teenagers to think in terms of frequencies rather than single-case probabilities—cut risky health decisions by nearly forty percent.[^1]

The third skill is *learning from feedback*. In the real world, decisions rarely come with clean feedback. If you choose a job and it goes badly, was it the wrong job, or did you execute poorly, or was it just bad luck? Without a structured way to parse outcomes, people repeat the same errors or, just as bad, abandon a perfectly good strategy after one failure. Decision education teaches a technique called “pre-mortem”: imagine that your decision turned out terribly, then work backwards to figure out why. It was popularized by the psychologist Gary Klein, and it forces you to surface hidden assumptions before you act. A study of MBA students at the Wharton School found that those who conducted a pre-mortem on a business case were 36% more likely to identify critical risks than those who didn’t.

Now, ask yourself: if you could only teach one skill to a young person entering the workforce, what would it be? Most people say “critical thinking” or “communication.” But those are umbrella terms. Decision education is the engine that powers them both. You can’t think critically without first framing the problem correctly. You can’t communicate a decision without understanding how you arrived at it.

The Alliance for Decision Education has already piloted its curriculum in several school districts, including in New Jersey and California. Early results are encouraging: students who went through a six-week decision course showed improvements not only in hypothetical choices but also in real-world outcomes like college persistence and reduced student debt. One district reported that students who took the course were 25% more likely to re-evaluate a major decision before finalizing it, compared to a control group.

But here’s the deeper point, and it’s the one that really keeps Fischhoff up at night. The current system doesn’t ignore decision education by accident. It ignores it because our model of success is built on the assumption that if you know enough facts, the right decision will follow. That assumption is wrong. The most knowledgeable people can make disastrous choices when they misweight probabilities or fail to consider alternative frames. The individual is not failing; the system has decided that this skill is not worth teaching.

And that brings us back to Fischhoff’s teenagers. They weren’t stupid. They could list every bad thing that could happen. They just couldn’t judge how likely those bad things were. A few hours of structured practice could have fixed that. We don’t give them those hours. Maybe we should start.

[^1]: The idea of teaching probability calibration to teenagers is older than you might think. In the 1980s, the psychologist Lola Lopes at the University of Wisconsin argued that risk perception was like a kind of “statistical literacy” that could be taught just as we teach reading. Her work was largely ignored by the education establishment, but it has found new life in the era of algorithmic decision-making.
