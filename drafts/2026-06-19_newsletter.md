SUBJECT LINE: Why Employee Ownership Alone Isn't Enough

Loren Rodgers, the executive director of the National Center for Employee Ownership, spends a lot of time staring at spreadsheets. His organization tracks thousands of companies that have transitioned to employee stock ownership plans, or ESOPs, and every year the numbers tell a curious story. Some of these companies explode. Productivity soars, profits climb, workers buy in. Others… don’t. They adopt the same ownership structure, hand out the same shares, and yet nothing changes. The culture stays flat. The business drifts. Rodgers once told me that the great puzzle of his field is not whether employee ownership works—we know it can—but *why* it works so inconsistently. “It’s like we’re giving people the keys to the car,” he said, “but forgetting to teach them how to drive.”

That metaphor is the heart of a new line of research that has been gaining traction over the past several years. The conventional wisdom, championed by everyone from management gurus to progressive politicians, holds that if you give employees a financial stake in the company, they will magically become more motivated and productive. It’s a beautiful theory. It also turns out to be incomplete. The missing piece, according to a growing body of work—including a recent article in *Inc.* by business consultant Bill Fotsch—is that ownership alone is not enough. You need two other ingredients: *economic engagement* (giving workers the information and authority to make real decisions) and *management engagement* (creating a culture where managers actually listen). Without those, employee ownership is just a check that never cashes.

But here’s the problem: that insight is not new. It was hiding in plain sight, nearly a century ago, in the story of one of the most famous employee-owned companies in the world.

In 1929, a young British businessman named John Spedan Lewis took over his father’s department store in London. The company was profitable, but Lewis was troubled by something. He noticed that the vast majority of the profits went to a few family members, while the workers—the people who actually sold the goods, stocked the shelves, kept the books—lived on modest wages. He thought this was fundamentally unfair. So he did something radical: he transferred ownership of the business to his employees. In 1929, the John Lewis Partnership was born, and Lewis drafted a constitution that declared the partnership’s purpose was “the happiness of its members.”

What happened next is fascinating. The company grew. It thrived. By the 1950s, it was a retail powerhouse, and the model was held up as a left-wing utopia come to life. But here’s the part of the story that often gets overlooked. Lewis did not stop at ownership. He also created a system of “democratic governance” that included elected councils, a dedicated grievance process, and something called the “Partnership’s weekly magazine” where any employee could submit a question or complaint—and management was required to publish the answer. He also insisted on open-book management: every year, the company published its financial results in plain language, and every employee was taught how to read a profit-and-loss statement. In other words, Lewis instinctively understood what modern research is now proving: ownership without engagement is hollow.

Take a moment and ask yourself: what would you do if you were given a piece of a company you didn’t understand and had no say in? Would you suddenly work harder? Probably not. That’s the puzzle Rodgers and his colleagues have been trying to solve. And their answer, supported by studies such as a 2018 analysis by Rutgers University and the NCEO, is that the combination of financial ownership, a voice in decision-making, and economic literacy is what drives performance. In one study, the productivity gains from employee ownership alone were modest. But when paired with a high-involvement management system and regular sharing of business information, the gains more than tripled.

That is the missing link—right there, in the data, and in the history of the John Lewis Partnership. For decades, we have been so enamored with the idea of giving workers a piece of the pie that we forgot to teach them how the pie is made. We handed them the keys, but we never showed them the engine.

So what does this mean for the modern company? It means that an ESOP on its own is not a silver bullet. It means that the CEO who thinks, “We set up an employee stock plan, now let’s sit back and watch the magic happen,” is likely to be disappointed. The real work is harder—and more interesting. It involves restructuring meetings so that frontline workers actually make decisions. It involves training managers to ask for input and then act on it. It involves teaching every single employee how a balance sheet works.[^1] That is hard. It is also, according to the research, exactly what separates the thriving employee-owned companies from the ones that just spin their wheels.

At the end of his day, Loren Rodgers still stares at spreadsheets. But now he looks for something new. He doesn’t just check the ownership structure. He looks for the companies that also score high on measures of employee involvement and financial transparency. “Those are the ones that jump off the page,” he told me. “They’re the ones where ownership actually matters.” John Spedan Lewis would have nodded.

Because a key is only useful if you know what door it opens.

[^1]: It is worth noting that "open-book management" has a separate intellectual lineage, championed by entrepreneur Jack Stack in the 1980s at his company SRC Holdings. Stack famously taught his factory workers to read income statements by turning them into a game. The overlap with the employee-ownership research is uncanny.
