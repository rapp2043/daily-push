SUBJECT LINE: The Wrong Way to Say You're Sorry

The customer service representative, let’s call her Maya, had been on the phone for forty-seven minutes. On the other end was a man whose new smart speaker had arrived with a deep scratch across its matte surface. Maya had listened, she had empathized, and she had apologized—repeatedly. “I’m so sorry for the inconvenience, sir.” “I do apologize for the frustration.” “We’re terribly sorry this happened.” Each iteration was meant to placate, to bridge the gap between expectation and reality. But with every “sorry,” the man’s voice grew tighter, his questions more pointed. By the time Maya issued the refund he was entitled to, the interaction was beyond salvage. He was promised a full credit, yet he hung up vowing never to buy from the company again. In her training, apologizing was the first and most sacred step. So why did it feel like pouring gasoline on a fire?

We live in a culture of apology. From corporate mea culpas to the reflexive “sorry!” when someone bumps into us, the act of expressing regret is seen as the social lubricant that smooths over friction. In business, this is codified dogma. “The customer is always right” is not just a motto; it’s an operational manual that begins with acknowledging fault. We are taught that an apology is a tool for rebuilding trust, a necessary offering to appease the wronged party. It signals humility, accountability, and a commitment to do better. It should be the perfect fix. But there’s a problem with that theory.

It turns out, in the delicate ecosystem of customer service, the apology is a far more volatile substance than we assume. Research is beginning to paint a curious picture: in many scenarios, saying “I’m sorry” can actually reduce trust, diminish satisfaction, and make the problem worse. This isn’t about insincerity. This is about the unexpected psychology of power, compensation, and the strange alchemy of what we think we want versus what actually helps us feel heard.

Consider the work of researchers like Adam Galinsky at Columbia Business School. In experiments on negotiation and conflict, a recurring theme emerges: apologies dynamically shift power. When one party apologizes, they often implicitly accept blame and lower their own status. In a balanced relationship, this can promote reconciliation. But in a classic customer service interaction—where the customer already feels vulnerable and one-down—the effect can be paradoxical. The company’s apology can inadvertently underscore the customer’s powerlessness. It reminds them they were wronged, it makes the grievance official, but it doesn’t, in itself, restore a sense of control. What the customer often desires isn’t an admission of guilt; it’s a restoration of agency. A solution is agency. An apology, by itself, is just a footnote to their frustration.[^1]

Then there is the complication of compensation. A study led by researchers including Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard Business School dug into this very puzzle. They examined interactions in contexts like airline delays. One group of service agents was trained to lead with an apology (“I’m sorry for the delay”). Another group was trained to lead with empathy but immediately pivot to the solution (“I see this delay has disrupted your plans; here’s what I can do for you right now”). The results were telling. Customers who received the immediate solution-oriented response reported higher satisfaction and greater trust in the company than those who received the upfront apology. The apology, it seemed, set a tone of defensiveness. It framed the interaction around the company’s failure. The solution, however, framed the interaction around the customer’s future. It was forward-looking. The apology anchored everyone in the past.

This brings us to the second counter-intuitive player in this drama: the non-apology apology, or what we might call the “pivot.” Think of the legendary customer service of Zappos. Their representatives are famous not for groveling, but for empowerment. Their training famously includes a core principle: don’t just follow a script to apologize; follow your empathy to solve. A story often repeated from their early days involves a customer who called to return shoes because her mother had died. The Zappos rep not only processed the return but, learning the customer would be attending funerals, sent a large bouquet of flowers to her home. Notice the structure of that story. There is no record of a protracted “I’m so sorry for your loss” from the company. There is, instead, an observation of need and an unexpected act of solution. The emotional resonance—and the legendary loyalty it created—came from the action, not the acknowledgment of fault. The empathy was embedded in the deed, not prefaced with a phrase.

But then we must confront the exception, the scenario where an apology is not just effective but essential. This is the realm of the deep, moral violation, where the wrong is about values, not value. When United Airlines dragged a passenger off an overbooked flight in 2017, the initial response from the CEO was a tepid statement defending employees. The outrage was volcanic. Why? Because the public perceived not just a service failure, but a betrayal of the basic compact of safety and dignity. In such cases, the commodity damaged is trust in the institution’s character. Here, a solution—a voucher, a refund—feels insultingly transactional. What is demanded is a full-throated, unequivocal apology that acknowledges the ethical breach. The apology *is* the first step of the solution because the problem is, at its heart, symbolic. The mistake companies make is using this high-stakes, moral-apology playbook for every minor service glitch. They cry wolf with “we are deeply sorry,” and when a true wolf appears, the words have lost all meaning.

So we are left with a peculiar map of human expectation. For the scratch on the speaker, the delayed flight, the incorrect charge—problems of convenience and contract—the human psyche seems to want something straightforward: a fix. A quick, fair, no-questions restoration of the world as it should be. The apology can feel like an unnecessary, even irritating, detour. It forces the aggrieved to perform emotional labor—to accept the apology, to appear mollified—before they’ve gotten what they came for.

Let’s play this out. You’re the customer. Your internet is down. You call. Two versions unfold. In the first, the agent says, “I’m terribly sorry for the outage. It’s our fault. We’re experiencing a network issue. We’re sorry.” You wait. In the second, the agent says, “I see your internet is down. That’s frustrating, especially if you’re working from home. I’ve already triggered a reset signal to your modem from here, and it should be back online in ninety seconds. We’re also crediting your account for today’s service.” Which interaction leaves you feeling more competent, more respected, more likely to stay with the company?

The answer, for most of us, is the second. The empathy was assumed, not stated. The focus was on agency and resolution. The apology was rendered obsolete by action.

Maya, our customer service rep, was following a script written for a different century of commerce, one where the mere acknowledgment of fault from a powerful corporation was a novelty. Today, we are buried in corporate apologies. They are the background noise of modern life. What cuts through the noise isn’t another “sorry.” It’s the quiet, efficient sound of a problem being solved. The customer may always be right, but what they are right about is their desire to stop being a customer with a problem, and to go back to being just a customer. Sometimes, the fastest way to get them there is to stop talking about what went wrong, and start building what happens next.

[^1]: The phrase “the customer is always right” is often attributed to Harry Gordon Selfridge, founder of the London department store, or to Marshall Field. Its intent was less about accepting blame and more about empowering floor staff to never argue with a shopper’s taste—to let the market decide. The moral interpretation, where it commands unilateral apology, is a later accretion.
