**SUBJECT LINE:** Why the behavioral economics you know is wrong

In 1985, Richard Thaler was a young economist with an office in Uris Hall at Cornell, trying to make sense of a puzzle. He had just finished a paper called “Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice”—a dry, four‑word title that hid something strange. Thaler had asked people a simple question: you’ve just spent ten dollars on a theater ticket, and on the way to the show you realize you’ve lost the ticket. Would you buy another? Most people said no. Then he asked a different group: you’re on your way to buy a ten‑dollar ticket, and you lose a ten‑dollar bill. Would you still buy the ticket? Most said yes. The logic was identical, but people treated the loss of the ticket and the loss of the cash as if they happened in two separate mental accounts. That, Thaler argued, made no sense under standard economics—but perfect sense under a new theory of human behavior he was trying to build.

It turns out that story is the one we think we know. Today, behavioral economics has been embraced by policymakers everywhere. Governments from London to Singapore have “nudge units.” [^1] The UK’s Behavioural Insights Team alone has been credited with everything from increasing tax compliance to boosting pension savings. The field is so mainstream that the 2017 Nobel Prize in Economics was awarded to Thaler himself. But there is a problem with that triumphant narrative. The version of behavioral economics that policymakers have embraced is not the one Thaler and his colleagues built at Cornell. It is a simpler, cleaner, and in some ways misleading version—and the gap between the two tells us something important about how ideas get flattened on their way to power.

Here are three things policymakers think they know about behavioral economics. And here is why each one is wrong.

First, they think it’s about quick fixes. The most famous tool in the behavioral toolkit is the “nudge”—a small tweak in the choice environment that steers people toward better decisions without removing their freedom. Auto‑enrollment in retirement plans is the classic example: when employees are automatically enrolled unless they opt out, participation rates skyrocket. In the UK, that single intervention added roughly £5 billion to pension savings in its first two years. But here is the uncomfortable truth that Thaler understood from the start: nudges work best on trivial choices. When you zoom in on the big stuff—financial literacy, long‑term health behavior, climate adaptation—the effect of a simple default fades. A 2019 review in *Behavioural Public Policy* found that while automatic enrollment certainly increases participation, it does almost nothing to increase the *amount* people save. The deeper irrationality—our inability to forecast our own future needs, our tendency to procrastinate on tough calculations—remains untouched. The Cornell version of behavioral economics was never about quick fixes. It was about a fundamental rethinking of what it means to be rational.

Second, policymakers think behavioral economics is a substitute for regulation. The phrase “libertarian paternalism,” coined by Thaler and his colleague Cass Sunstein in their 2008 book *Nudge*, was meant to suggest a middle path: guide choices, don’t command them. But what began as a complement quickly became a crutch. Before long, governments were using nudges to avoid making hard regulatory decisions. Consider the case of the UK’s sugar tax, which was famously delayed for years while the government experimented with calorie labels and “traffic‑light” warnings on food packages. The nudges did move the needle slightly, but the real drop in sugar consumption came only after the tax was introduced in 2018. Sunstein himself has acknowledged the limits: “Nudges are not a substitute for bans or incentives when the stakes are high,” he wrote in a 2014 paper. Yet in practice, they have often been used exactly that way. The Cornell insight was that people need structure—not just gentle taps, but guardrails. Thaler’s early work on self‑control problems showed that people actually *want* to restrict their own future choices, the way Odysseus tied himself to the mast. That is a far cry from a sticker on a soda bottle.

Third, policymakers think behavioral economics is universal. This is the most dangerous assumption of all. Many of the foundational studies in the field were conducted on small, WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) populations—often university students at Cornell, or subjects recruited in the lobby of a psychology department at the University of British Columbia. The famous experiment on “loss aversion,” where people hate losing twenty dollars more than they enjoy gaining twenty, was replicated dozens of times—but almost always in rich countries with stable economies. When researchers tried to replicate those same studies in rural India or parts of sub‑Saharan Africa, the results were sometimes reversed. A 2016 paper in *Science* by a large consortium of behavioral scientists found that the effect sizes of many classic nudges shrank dramatically when tested across different cultures and contexts. [^2] The nudge that works in a London pension office may fail in a Jakarta market. The Cornell version of behavioral economics was built on careful, context‑bound experiments; the policy version pretends the context doesn’t matter. That is not just sloppy—it is unscientific.

So what are we to make of this? The story we tell about behavioral economics—born at Cornell, embraced by policymakers, a triumph of light‑touch government—is not entirely false. It is just incomplete. The real insight, buried in Thaler’s 1985 desk drawer, is that humans are complicated, contradictory, and deeply predictable in their irrationality. You cannot fix that with a single default. You cannot automate it with a smartphone app. And you cannot export it from Ithaca to Indonesia and expect the same result.

When Thaler wrote that first mental‑accounting paper, he ended with a sentence that sounds almost humble today: “The challenge for economists is to develop a more complete theory of choice, one that can account for the observed discrepancies.” The challenge for policymakers is not to use behavioral economics as a magic wand—but to finally take it seriously. That means acknowledging that the tool is limited, that the environment matters, and that sometimes the only honest intervention is the one the policymakers would rather not make.

[^1]: A personal note: the UK’s Behavioural Insights Team was originally housed inside the Cabinet Office, but it was spun off into a private company in 2014. The irony of a government trying to “nudge” its own citizens while simultaneously privatizing the very unit designed to do the nudging has not been lost on critics. But that is a story for another day.

[^2]: The replication was part of the “Many Labs” project, a massive effort to test the robustness of psychological findings. The loss‑aversion effect, for all its fame, did not replicate equally well across cultures. The authors noted that “contextual factors may moderate the strength of loss aversion”—academic language for: we don’t actually know why it works when it works.
