**SUBJECT LINE:** The Secret Civil War Inside Cultural Studies

In the mid-1990s, a professor named John Storey sat down to do something seemingly straightforward. He wanted to answer a simple question: *What is cultural studies?* His approach was to collect essays from the field’s most prominent thinkers—Stuart Hall, Raymond Williams, Angela McRobbie, and others—and present them in a single volume. That volume, *What is Cultural Studies? A Reader*, was published in 1996 by Routledge. You would expect a book with that title to offer a clear, unified definition. Instead, what Storey ended up with was something closer to a family feud.

The contributors—many of them colleagues, some of them former students of each other—could not agree on the most basic questions. Was cultural studies a discipline? An interdisciplinary project? A political movement? One essay argued that cultural studies should be rooted in Marxism; the next insisted that Marxism was exactly the kind of grand narrative the field needed to escape. And there, on page after page, was a contradiction: a field that had spent decades claiming to study power, resistance, and hegemony was itself a battleground of competing egos and theories. The very thing that made cultural studies so compelling—its refusal to settle down—was also the thing that made it impossible to define.

It turns out that this is not unusual. In fact, it’s a pattern that shows up in almost any field that tries to be both academic and political. But cultural studies is a particularly vivid example, because the fights were never just about ideas. They were about people.

Let’s look at the first major split. On one side were the old guard, the British founders who had come out of the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies. Led by figures like Richard Hoggart and the early Stuart Hall, they believed that cultural studies should be a tool for understanding how ordinary people resist power. Their method was ethnographic, their politics Marxist. On the other side were the post-structuralists, influenced by French theorists like Michel Foucault and Jacques Derrida, who argued that power was everywhere and nowhere, that resistance was itself a product of the system. This wasn’t a polite academic disagreement. It was a war over the soul of the field. At one memorable conference in the 1980s, a younger scholar stood up and accused Hall of being too deterministic. Hall, never one to back down, fired back that the post-structuralists were surrendering to relativism.[^1]

Now, here is a question for you. Imagine you are a graduate student at that conference. You have been taught that cultural studies is a liberation project, a way to give voice to the marginalized. But now you hear two of its most famous figures shouting at each other about whether the very concept of “truth” exists. What do you do? Do you pick a side? Do you walk out? This is exactly the kind of tension that Storey’s reader captures—not a clean answer, but a messy, living argument.

A second fault line ran along geography. The British version of cultural studies was rooted in class analysis. Richard Hoggart’s *The Uses of Literacy* (1957) was a study of working-class life in post-war England. Raymond Williams’ *Culture and Society* (1958) traced the evolution of the word “culture” itself from a noun meaning “cultivation” to a noun meaning “a whole way of life.” In Britain, cultural studies was about history, about tradition, about the struggle of the working class. But when cultural studies crossed the Atlantic, it became something else. In the United States, the field was taken up by literature departments and film schools, and it quickly became obsessed with identity—race, gender, sexuality. The Americans wanted to talk about difference; the British wanted to talk about class. They looked at each other with a mixture of curiosity and contempt. The result was a series of essays in Storey’s reader that, when read side by side, feel like they are describing two entirely different subjects.

A third dispute was about the very object of study. Should cultural studies focus on popular culture—movies, music, television—or should it also examine the institutions that produce culture? Academics like Stuart Hall argued that you couldn’t understand *Dallas* without understanding the television industry. Others, like the American critic John Fiske, believed that audiences were so creative and resistant that the industry barely mattered. Again, two valid positions, but completely incompatible.

So what are we to make of all this? At first glance, the Storey reader seems to be a failure. It set out to answer a simple question and ended up showing that there was no answer. But that’s precisely the point. Cultural studies is not a unified discipline; it is a collection of overlapping, often contradictory conversations. That is not a weakness—it is a strength. The best ideas, like the most interesting people, are never completely consistent. They contain contradictions that push them forward.

Storey himself acknowledged this in his introduction. He wrote that cultural studies is “a discursive formation, a specific way of thinking about culture that is always in process.” In other words, it is a living thing. And living things do not stand still. They squabble, they evolve, they surprise you.

The next time someone tells you that cultural studies is a single, coherent field of inquiry, ask them to open Storey’s reader. They will find, in the margins of those yellowed pages, the fingerprints of a hundred intellectual fights. And they will realize that the real story of cultural studies is not about what it is, but about what it is not—and how that very instability has made it one of the most productive ways of thinking about the modern world.

[^1]: This exchange is documented in several histories of cultural studies, including *Stuart Hall: Conversations, Projects and Legacies* (2017) and is a well-known anecdote among scholars of the field.

[^2]: For a more detailed account of the British-American divide, see *Cultural Studies in the United States* by Janice Radway, published in *Cultural Studies* (1993).

[^3]: The “audience resistance” debate between Fiske and his critics is summarized in *Understanding Popular Culture* by John Fiske (1989) and the responses in *The Audience Studies Reader* (2003).
