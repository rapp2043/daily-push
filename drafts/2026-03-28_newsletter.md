SUBJECT LINE: The Winner's Curse: Why Every Victory Plants a Seed of Dread

On a damp, grey afternoon in May 1954, a 25-year-old medical student named Roger Bannister did the impossible. On the cinder track at Oxford’s Iffley Road, he ran a mile in under four minutes—a barrier considered not just a physical limit, but a psychological one. The world erupted. Bannister was catapulted to a fame so intense it had a physical weight. But in the quiet moments after the roar faded, a different sensation crept in. It wasn’t exhaustion from the effort, but a cold, gnawing dread about what came next. He had reached the summit. There was only one direction to go from a summit. In interviews later, he would articulate the peculiar burden of that day: the moment of his greatest triumph was also the moment he became a prisoner of expectation.[^1] The world now watched, waiting for him to do it again, or to fail spectacularly trying. He had conquered the mountain, only to find himself perched on a narrow, terrifying ledge.

We assume success is a salve. It’s the finish line, the trophy, the definitive answer to the question of our worth. We believe that once you’ve proven yourself—once you’ve landed the big client, published the bestselling novel, made the perfect launch—the anxiety evaporates. You’ve earned the right to relax. The fear of failure, that constant companion on the climb, should finally be dismissed. But here’s the problem with that theory. For a staggering number of people who achieve remarkable things, the opposite happens. The fear doesn’t vanish; it mutates. It grows louder, more sophisticated, and infinitely more paralyzing. This is the success paradox: the higher you climb, the more terrifying the view of the ground below becomes.

So what does this look like in the wild? It’s the tenured professor who, after finally securing permanent job security, is paralyzed by the thought that her next paper won’t be “groundbreaking” enough. It’s the founder who, after a successful IPO, lies awake worrying that the next quarterly report will reveal him as a fraud. Research in organizational psychology and behavioral science consistently circles this phenomenon. Studies on what’s termed “impostor syndrome” find it is frequently most acute among high achievers—those with a shelf of accolades to supposedly disprove the feeling. Their success becomes not evidence of their capability, but a higher-stakes arena where they might finally be exposed. The data, in a sense, becomes meaningless against the louder data of their own anxiety.

This brings us to our central puzzle. Logically, success should provide empirical proof of competence. It should build a buffer of confidence so thick that minor setbacks bounce off. Why, then, does it so often have the perverse effect of making people more fragile, more cautious, and more haunted by the specter of failure?

To understand this, we need to shift our perspective on what success actually *does* to a person’s environment and identity. The crucial twist is that success doesn’t simplify your life; it complexifies it. Before the breakthrough, your identity is diffuse. You are an “aspiring novelist” or a “striving entrepreneur.” The canvas is blank, and every stroke is potential. But a major success acts like a branding iron. It sharply defines you. You are now “the author of *X*,” “the CEO who turned around *Y*.” Your world, which was once wide with possibility, narrows to a single, well-lit corridor you are expected to walk down forever. Every subsequent action is now measured against the towering benchmark of your past achievement. The freedom of the unknown is replaced by the tyranny of the known.

Consider the world of professional athletics, a pure laboratory for this paradox. A tennis player spends her youth dreaming of winning a Grand Slam. The struggle is all-consuming. Then she wins Wimbledon. The elation is real, but it is often swiftly followed by a profound disorientation. The quest that organized her entire existence—the early mornings, the brutal training, the single-minded focus—is over. She achieved the thing. Now what? The next tournament is no longer about chasing a dream; it’s about defending a title. The narrative flips from hopeful ascent to precarious defense. Every match becomes a test not of her skill, but of her legitimacy. The fear of losing what she has gained can become a more powerful motivator—and a more potent source of paralysis—than the desire to gain it ever was.[^2]

This phenomenon isn’t confined to individuals; it sculpts the behavior of entire organizations. Look at the history of technology. A company achieves a breakthrough success—a dominant operating system, a revolutionary search engine, a ubiquitous social network. The initial culture of daring innovation, of risking failure on a dozen crazy ideas to find one golden one, slowly hardens. The stakes are now unimaginably high. Shareholders expect growth. The market watches every move. Failure is no longer a private lesson; it’s a public headline that can shatter billions in market value. So what happens? The company often becomes risk-averse. It starts managing and protecting its success rather than continuing to gamble for the next one. It begins to fear failure so deeply that it inadvertently sows the seeds of its own stagnation. The very thing they are terrified of—becoming obsolete—is made more likely by their terror of making a misstep.

Let’s play a quick thought experiment. Imagine you are offered your dream job tomorrow. The title you’ve coveted, the salary you’ve fantasized about, the office with the window. Now, in that first moment of private celebration, what is the very next thought that flashes, unbidden, across your mind? For many, it isn’t pure joy. It’s a question: “Can I actually do this?” Or worse: “What happens when they find out I can’t?” This is the success paradox in its raw, immediate form. The opportunity feels less like a reward and more like a test you might fail.

The mechanism here is what we might call “identity entanglement.” When your sense of self becomes fused with a specific achievement, that achievement is no longer something you *did*; it becomes who you *are*. Your worth is contingent on maintaining that altitude. Any stumble isn’t just a mistake; it’s an identity crisis. This is why praise can be so double-edged. Telling someone “You’re a natural genius” after a success can be more damaging than telling them “You worked really hard.” The “genius” label is a static identity to live up to. The “hard work” label is a process to continue. One sets a trap; the other builds a path.

So where does this leave Roger Bannister on that grey Oxford day? He had just become “The Man Who Broke the Four-Minute Mile.” It was a thrilling identity, but also a confining one. Every race he ran thereafter would be a referendum on that identity. He understood, intuitively, that his relationship with running had changed forever in the moment he crossed that line. The pure, striving chase was over. What remained was the burden of legacy. He retired from competitive running just a year later, turning his focus fully to his medical career in neurology—a field where he could begin a new climb, free from the paralyzing shadow of his own peak.

The universal lesson here is not that success is bad, but that it is misunderstood. We think of it as a destination, a place of rest and security. In reality, it is often a transition into a more psychologically complex game, where the stakes are invisibly raised and the rules are written in fear. Success doesn’t eliminate the fear of failure. It refines it, personalizes it, and gives it a megaphone. The challenge, then, is not to avoid success, but to build an identity spacious enough that a single achievement doesn’t fill the whole room. You are not your trophy. You are the person who sometimes wins them, and sometimes learns from not winning them. The goal is to keep the canvas wide, even after you’ve painted a masterpiece in one corner. The only way off that narrow ledge is to remember that the valley below isn’t a grave; it’s just another part of the landscape.

[^1]: Bannister’s relationship with his achievement was famously complex. He often emphasized the team effort behind the run and expressed relief at moving on to his medical career. He saw the four-minute mile as an accomplishment, but not the defining event of his life, a perspective that may have been his psychological salvation.
[^2]: This dynamic explains the often-observed “Sophomore Slump” in everything from literature to music to sports. The first success is an explosion of unchecked potential. The second act is created under the blinding spotlight of expectation, where every choice is scrutinized against the beloved original. The fear of failing to meet that standard can stifle the very creativity that created it.
