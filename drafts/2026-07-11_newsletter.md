SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Gut Feelings Are Just Math in Disguise

In 2011, the Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman published *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Buried in the middle of the book is a simple puzzle. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? If you answered ten cents, you are wrong, but you are also in excellent company. Most people give that answer. The real answer is five cents. The puzzle is a perfect illustration of Kahneman’s central idea: our intuitive System 1 thinking is fast, automatic, and prone to systematic errors. The bat-and-ball puzzle isn’t a trick—it’s a window into the architecture of human judgment.

For decades, that’s how we understood decision-making. Through puzzles, heuristics, and a small set of equations—prospect theory, temporal discounting, the endowment effect. The math was elegant but limited. A few well-chosen numbers could explain why people prefer a certain small gain over a risky larger one, but they couldn’t capture the messy richness of real-world decisions.

And then something strange happened. The same technology that powers chatbots began to be used to map the human mind. Specifically, researchers started combining LLMs—large language models like the one behind ChatGPT—with mathematical models of decision-making. The result? A new way of seeing how we decide. Not as isolated errors or biases, but as a kind of hidden geography. A map of the decision-making landscape that is startlingly precise.

Consider three distinct layers of this work.

The first layer is the classical mathematical approach, which stretches back to the cold calculations of John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern’s expected utility theory. Later, Kahneman and his collaborator Amos Tversky redrew the map with prospect theory, showing that people weigh losses and gains asymmetrically. Their S-shaped value function is pure math: a curve that explains why losing $100 hurts twice as much as winning $100 feels good. That curve is still the foundation. It tells us that human choice is not random; it follows predictable, quantifiable patterns. But it’s a very coarse map, like a 17th-century globe with huge blank spaces labeled *Here Be Dragons* [^1].

The second layer is the data-driven approach using large language models. Instead of starting with equations, researchers feed LLMs thousands of decision tasks—catalogues of choices made by real people in experiments. The model learns the underlying structure. It becomes a kind of statistical shadow of the human mind. In one recent study described by Neuroscience News, researchers used an LLM to simulate how people would choose between gambles, risky options, and delayed rewards. The model didn’t just match human performance; it revealed hidden patterns that even the original experimenters hadn’t noticed. For instance, people who seemed irrational under one theory turned out to be perfectly consistent when their choices were seen through the lens of the LLM’s latent representations [^2].

But here’s where it gets interesting. The third layer—the truly powerful one—is the combination of the first two. Researchers are now fusing the mathematical models (like prospect theory) with the pattern-recognition abilities of LLMs. The math provides the skeleton; the LLM fills in the flesh. The result is a hybrid map that is both interpretable and incredibly accurate. It can tell you not just that someone will avoid a risky gamble, but *why*: maybe because of loss aversion, or maybe because of a subtle framing effect that only an LLM could detect. The two approaches correct each other’s blind spots. The math gives the model a principled structure, preventing overfitting. The LLM gives the math flexibility to account for the bewildering variety of real human choices.

Here’s a thought experiment. Imagine you are a consumer psychologist for a company that wants to predict whether a shopper will buy a product. You have the classic economic model: price, income, utility. It’s okay, but it misses emotions, context, and word-of-mouth. Now imagine you also have a language model that has read millions of product reviews and conversations. If you combine them—a math model of price sensitivity plus an LLM of sentiment and narrative—you get a prediction that is not just better, but qualitatively different. You can see the decision tree branching across both rational and emotional dimensions.

I would like to run a test with you. Take a moment and ask yourself what you think happens next. If you map the decision-making of a thousand people this way, what do you get? Not a thousand isolated minds, but a unified landscape where everyone’s choices are variations on a few deep themes. That’s the real discovery. The research described in the recent *Neuroscience News* article suggests that by combining LLMs with math, we can now see that human decision-making is not a collection of quirky biases but a coherent system governed by hidden laws.

Remember that bat and ball? Ten cents is the intuitive answer, and it’s wrong. But with our new map, we don’t just see the error. We see the exact shape of the mental shortcut that produced it. We see that the shortcut is a compressed version of a deeper probabilistic reasoning that, under different circumstances, would be correct. The bat and ball puzzle is not a bug—it’s a feature of a mind optimized for speed. And now we can finally see the whole machine.

The unseen advantage of combining LLMs and math is that we stop treating each decision as a mysterious inner choice and start seeing it as a predictable outcome of a system. The system is not the individual. The system is the *landscape* of human cognition, a terrain that is finally being mapped in full [^3].

---

[^1]: The early mathematical models of decision-making—like expected utility—were so clean they seemed almost beautiful. But they assumed humans were perfectly rational. When Kahneman and Tversky showed that real people behaved differently, many economists were distressed. Now we know that the irrationalities themselves follow mathematical laws.

[^2]: Large language models are trained on enormous corpora of human text, which means they carry within them a kind of statistical portrait of human thought. But they are not conscious. They don't *decide*—they predict. That’s exactly why they are useful for mapping decision-making: they are mirrors that reflect our patterns back at us.

[^3]: Some critics worry that this kind of mapping could be used for manipulation—predicting and nudging people against their will. That’s a real concern. But the map itself is neutral. What matters is who reads it.
