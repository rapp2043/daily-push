**SUBJECT LINE:** Why Your Gym Can’t Reach Autistic Clients (It’s Not What You Think)

When James was eight, his parents did what any parents would do: they signed him up for soccer. He loved to run—really loved it, the way some kids love ice cream or dinosaurs. But after three practices, the coach pulled his mother aside. “He’s not paying attention,” the coach said. “He doesn’t follow the plays. Maybe he’s not ready for team sports.” James didn’t get a second chance. His parents assumed he just wasn’t interested in physical activity. And for years, that assumption stuck.

That story—or one like it—plays out in homes and schools across the country. There is a deeply held belief that autistic individuals, as a group, have little interest in exercise, or that their sensory differences make movement uncomfortable, or that they lack the coordination or social motivation to join in. It’s a tidy explanation for why so many autistic people are less physically active than their neurotypical peers. But it’s wrong.

A recent report—covered by Medical Xpress in August 2025[^1]—took a hard look at this assumption. Researchers at the University of Virginia[^2] set out to answer a simple question: What really keeps autistic adults and children away from exercise? Their answer was startling. It wasn’t a lack of desire. It wasn’t even the sensory challenges people assume. The biggest barrier was something far more mundane—and fixable.

Before I tell you what they found, do a quick thought experiment. Imagine you are an autistic person who wants to get fit. You walk into a gym. The music is loud, the lights are harsh, the floor plan is a maze of machines. A trainer approaches and launches into a rapid-fire explanation of circuit training. You ask for clarification. He speaks *louder*. You try to explain that you need to see a diagram. He shrugs. Now ask yourself: Do you come back tomorrow?

Most people would say no. But we tend to blame the individual—*they just don’t like exercise*. The study flips that. The researchers surveyed fitness professionals and autistic adults, and what they found was a deep mismatch in understanding. Over three-quarters of autistic participants described fitness environments as unwelcoming. The number one reason? Staff members didn’t know how to communicate with them or adapt activities. In other words, the problem wasn’t the autistic person’s motivation. It was the professional’s training.

Take another story, this one from the same research. A young woman named Emily[^3] had always wanted to take yoga. She tried a class once, but the instructor moved through poses so quickly that Emily couldn’t keep up. When she asked the instructor to slow down and explain the alignment, the instructor told her to “just listen to your body.” That phrase—so common in wellness culture—means almost nothing to someone who processes sensory information differently. Emily left and didn’t come back. But she didn’t leave because she hated yoga. She left because the class wasn’t built for her.

The data from the study makes the pattern clear: when fitness professionals received even basic training on neurodiversity, participation rates among autistic clients rose dramatically. It was a simple intervention. Visual schedules, breaking down instructions into single steps, allowing time to process, minimizing noise. None of this is expensive or exotic. But it almost never happens.

So why do we keep assuming the problem is the autistic person? This brings us to the core theory: we have a tendency to attribute behavior to disposition rather than circumstance. When a neurotypical kid avoids a sport, we ask what’s wrong with the sport. When an autistic kid avoids it, we ask what’s wrong with the kid. It’s a classic attribution error—and it has been reinforced by decades of research that focused on deficits rather than environments. The new study challenges that framework. It suggests that the physical activity gap is not a health problem belonging to autistic individuals. It is an inclusion problem belonging to everyone else.

Now, back to James. His parents eventually found an adapted swim program run by a coach who had studied how to teach autistic swimmers. The coach used picture cards. He explained each stroke one step at a time. He turned down the music. James didn’t just swim—he became a competitive swimmer, winning races in his age group. The only thing that had changed was the environment.

That is the surprising truth. We have spent years worrying about whether autistic people can exercise. The real question is whether we are willing to change how we offer it.
