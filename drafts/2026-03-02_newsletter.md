SUBJECT LINE: Why the Best Leaders Never Choose

Wendy Smith is explaining paradoxes to a room of future CEOs, and she starts with a story that sounds like a riddle. It’s the early 2000s, and a young, fast-growing tech company is tearing itself apart. The engineers are screaming for more freedom to experiment, to chase the next big idea. The sales team is demanding process, reliability, and a product they can actually sell next quarter. The founding CEO, caught in the middle, feels he must pick a side: discipline *or* creativity. He chooses discipline. The company becomes efficient, predictable, and within a few years, utterly irrelevant. Smith leans in. “The mistake,” she tells her quiet classroom, “was thinking it was an ‘or.’” The secret wasn’t in the choice, but in refusing to make it.

We are taught that good leadership is about clarity. Decisiveness. A clear vision, communicated in straightforward directives. Pick a strategy and execute. This logic is embedded in every business book, every management seminar. Inconsistency is a sign of weakness; waffling is fatal. But here’s the problem with that theory: it doesn’t match the world the most successful leaders actually inhabit. The world isn’t a series of clear either/or decisions. It’s a tangled knot of “both/and.” The real challenge isn’t choosing between competing demands—innovation *or* efficiency, globalization *or* local focus, today’s profit *or* tomorrow’s purpose—but figuring out how to pursue them simultaneously.

This is the central puzzle of modern leadership. Why do some leaders and organizations thrive amidst contradiction, while others, seeking clarity above all else, stagnate?

Smith, a management scholar at the University of Delaware, has spent her career studying what she and her colleagues call “paradox theory.” The core idea is elegant in its simplicity: the most persistent, vexing tensions in organizations aren’t problems to be solved. They are dynamos to be harnessed.[^1] A paradox, in this sense, isn’t a logical fallacy. It’s a pair of opposing elements that are interdependent and persist over time. You can’t have creativity without some structure to channel it. You can’t have stability without the capacity for change. They need each other.

Research in this field suggests a startling reversal of conventional wisdom. Studies of top-performing teams and companies consistently find they don’t resolve tensions; they lean into them. They build systems that actively nurture opposing capabilities. One landmark body of work out of Stanford highlighted how the most innovative companies maintained rigorous operational routines *alongside* protected spaces for unscripted experimentation. The routine operations funded the wild experiments, and the occasional breakthrough from the experiments renewed the routines. It was a single, self-reinforcing system.

Consider a very different arena: NASA. For decades after the Apollo triumph, NASA was the embodiment of disciplined, hierarchical, process-engineering excellence. And then, in 2003, the Space Shuttle Columbia disintegrated on re-entry. The subsequent investigation revealed a culture that had become too good at one thing: following rules. Concerns from engineers about the foam strike during launch were meticulously processed through proper channels, but the channels were designed for certainty, not for ambiguity. The system had lost its capacity to hold the paradox of “routine procedure” and “vigilant doubt.” They had chosen order, and it killed them.[^2]

Now, let’s play a quick game. You’re the CEO of a century-old manufacturing company. Your core business is steady, but it’s slowly eroding. Do you: A) Double down on your historic strengths, cutting costs and optimizing your legacy products to squeeze out every last penny? Or B) Bet the farm on a radical new technology that could make your old business obsolete? Most leadership frameworks force this choice. But the work of scholars like Michael Tushman at Harvard shows the answer is neither. The answer is to do both, but in deliberately separate ways. You create a “ambidextrous organization.” One arm ruthlessly manages the declining core, extending its life and cash flow. A separate arm, with different people, different metrics, and different rules, operates like a startup to build the future. They are in constant tension, competing for resources and attention. And that tension is the source of the company’s vitality and longevity.

This is the subtle shift in mindset that Smith teaches. It’s not about compromise, finding a mushy middle ground between innovation and efficiency. That leads to mediocrity. It’s about *actively pursuing* both extremes. It’s about building an organization smart enough and confident enough to house competing truths. The leader’s job is not to be the decider, but the designer of the container that can hold the heat of that contradiction.

So we return to Wendy Smith’s classroom. The future CEO who learns her lesson won’t walk into a boardroom and say, “Here’s my clear, simple plan.” They’ll say, “Here are the two most important, contradictory things we must excel at. Here’s how we’re going to set up teams to chase each one with full passion. And here’s how I’m going to manage the creative friction between them.” They understand that the power isn’t in the choice between two paths, but in the sustained, dynamic tension of walking both at once. The chaos isn’t a sign of failure; it’s the hidden order of things that last.

[^1]: The academic definition of a "paradox" here is crucial. It's not merely a contradiction or a puzzle, but elements that "seem logical in isolation but absurd, irrational, or contradictory when combined." Scholars like Smith argue that treating them as mere problems to be "solved" misses their generative, enduring nature.
[^2]: The Columbia Accident Investigation Board report was scathing on this cultural point. It noted that NASA’s history of success had fostered a "blind spot" regarding the need for both formal process and informal vigilance. The system became so good at the former it extinguished the latter.
