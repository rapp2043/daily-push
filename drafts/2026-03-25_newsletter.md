SUBJECT LINE: What If Everything We Know About Human Violence Is Wrong?

The anthropologist Richard Wrangham was deep in the Congo Basin, watching a group of bonobos, when he saw something that would quietly begin to unravel a story he himself had helped tell. It was the 1990s, and Wrangham, along with many of his colleagues, was steeped in a certain view of human evolution. We were the descendants of the more aggressive, more violent apes. Our lineage was paved with conflict. The evidence seemed to be written in the bones of our ancestors and the behavior of our closest living relatives, the chimpanzees. But here, in this other part of the jungle, were the bonobos—our other closest relatives—and they were different. They were playful, sexual, and markedly less murderous. Wrangham, co-author of the influential book *Demonic Males*, had helped cement the idea of a deep evolutionary legacy of male aggression. But the bonobos presented a puzzle. They were a living counter-argument, swinging through the trees.[^1]

The conventional wisdom, the one Wrangham helped propagate, is seductive in its simplicity. It says that war, rape, and murder are in our blood. We point to the brutal warfare of chimpanzees, to archaeological sites littered with bludgeoned skulls, to the endless chronicle of human history itself. The narrative is clear: civilization is a thin veneer stretched over a core of primal savagery. We are, as the philosopher Thomas Hobbes famously declared, in a state of nature where life is “nasty, brutish, and short.” This idea has shaped everything from our politics to our movie plots. It feels instinctively true. But here’s the problem with that theory: it might be built on a profound misreading of the evidence.

It turns out that when you scrub away the assumptions and look at the data with fresh eyes, the story of innate, pervasive human violence begins to crack. Take those ancient bones. For decades, archaeologists pointed to sites like Jebel Sahaba in Sudan, a 13,000-year-old cemetery where many skeletons showed signs of violent trauma, as proof of constant prehistoric warfare. It became a textbook example. But a recent reanalysis, using modern forensic techniques, tells a different tale. The injuries weren’t all from a single battle; they accumulated over centuries, even millennia. The evidence suggests sporadic, small-scale conflict, not a state of endless war. It looks less like the Normandy beaches and more like the slow, tragic toll of long-running family feuds. The “brutish” past starts to look more nuanced, more human.

And then there are the living counterexamples. For generations, anthropologists went into the field expecting to find violence. And, often, they found it. But what about the societies where they didn’t? The Semai of Malaysia, studied by Robert Knox Dentan, were so non-violent that they lacked even a word for “war.” Violence was considered so bizarre and tragic it was thought to be the result of spirit possession. The Inuit of Greenland had complex conflict-resolution rituals designed to dissolve anger through song and laughter, not bloodshed. For a long time, these cultures were treated as curious exceptions, peaceful Edens that proved the violent rule. But what if we’ve had it backwards? What if they aren’t the exceptions, but vivid demonstrations of a different human potential?

Here’s a thought experiment. Imagine two food trucks setting up on opposite ends of a city park. One day, a customer gets food poisoning from Truck A. Word spreads. Soon, Truck A is out of business, ruined by reputation. Truck B thrives. Now, was the owner of Truck A inherently, biologically predisposed to fail? Of course not. A specific, contingent event—a bad batch of mayonnaise—cascaded into a structural outcome. This is what some researchers argue we see with human societies. Violence isn’t a default setting; it’s often a tool, a response to specific, contingent pressures: scarcity, fear, social inequality, or the invention of new, more lethal technologies.

The research challenging the “killer ape” theory has been accumulating. A major review published in the journal *Nature* in 2016, which sifted through data from hunter-gatherer societies—the closest analog we have to our ancestral state—concluded that lethal aggression was relatively rare until the dawn of agriculture and settled societies. It was social and material conditions that escalated violence, not a sudden eruption of our inner beast. Another scholar, R. Brian Ferguson, has spent years meticulously documenting how contact with expanding colonial states often triggered or intensified warfare in indigenous societies. The violence wasn’t bubbling up from within; it was often a reaction to a profound, destabilizing shock from without.

So, what are we to make of this? The revelation is this: we are not prisoners of a violent biology. Our capacity for aggression is real, but it is just that—a capacity, not a destiny. The bonobos show one evolutionary path focused on empathy and cooperation. The chimpanzees show another. We contain both possibilities. The cultures we’ve built—from the war-torn to the peaceful—are not expressions of an immutable inner nature. They are choices, amplifications of one set of tendencies over another. We built the state, the army, the standing stockpile of weapons. But we also built the jury trial, the mediation center, and the truth and reconciliation commission.

Which brings us back to Richard Wrangham. In recent years, he has undergone a quiet but profound shift. He now focuses less on the “demonic” and more on what he calls the “goodness paradox.” Yes, humans can be capable of horrific violence, but we are also uniquely capable of *proactive, coordinated* non-violence—of banding together to suppress the bullies among us. Our evolutionary gift, he now argues, isn’t a taste for blood; it’s our ability to conspire against tyranny, to police our own societies, and to enforce cooperation. The story of human evolution isn’t the story of the club. It’s the story of the gossiping, plotting, peacemaking group that decided, together, to take the club away. We are not inherently violent. We are inherently *judges*. And that changes everything.

[^1]: Bonobos are often called the "hippie apes" for their use of sexual contact to ease social tensions and resolve conflicts. Their societies are matriarchal, which some researchers link to their lower levels of aggression compared to male-dominated chimpanzee troops.
