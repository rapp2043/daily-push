SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Best Decisions Are Made in Seconds

In the early 1990s, a fire captain in Ohio arrived at what seemed like a routine kitchen fire. The flames were small, the smoke was light, and his men were ready to go in. But something felt wrong. The captain couldn’t articulate what it was—he just had a strange, sinking feeling in his gut. He ordered his crew to stay out. A few seconds later, the floor collapsed. Had they gone in, the entire team would have been swallowed by a hidden inferno in the basement. That captain later told the cognitive psychologist Gary Klein that he had no idea why he gave the order. He just *knew*.

We like to think that important decisions require careful deliberation. We weigh pros and cons, make lists, sleep on it. That’s the responsible thing to do. But there’s a problem with that theory: sometimes—maybe even most of the time—the best decision comes not from careful thought, but from its absence.

Consider a famous experiment conducted by the Dutch psychologist Ap Dijksterhuis and his colleagues. They gave participants information about four different cars—attributes like gas mileage, legroom, and reliability. One car was objectively superior. Some participants were asked to deliberate for four minutes before choosing. Others were given a distraction—a word puzzle—and then forced to choose immediately. Which group picked the better car? The distracted group. In a 2006 study published in *Science*, Dijksterhuis found that for complex decisions, unconscious thought—thought that happens when we’re not consciously focusing on the problem—outperforms conscious deliberation. The brain continues to process information in the background, integrating details we didn’t even know we noticed.

Now, you might be thinking: *But that’s just choosing a car. What about big, life-altering decisions?* The same pattern holds. Sheena Iyengar, a professor at Columbia Business School, studied how people choose among dozens of jams at a gourmet market. When shoppers saw a display of 24 jams, only 3 percent bought a jar. When the display was cut to six jams, 30 percent bought. More information, more options, more thinking—it didn’t lead to better choices. It led to paralysis and regret. The shoppers who bought from the small display were happier with their purchase. In a 2000 paper, Iyengar and Mark Lepper showed that the conventional wisdom—that more choice is always better—is flat wrong.

So here’s a puzzle: if conscious deliberation often fails, how do we make good decisions? The answer, according to a growing body of research, involves something called *heuristics*—simple mental shortcuts that are fast and surprisingly accurate. The German psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer has spent decades showing that in many situations, a single good reason can beat a complex analysis. For instance, he found that emergency room doctors who used a simple decision tree to diagnose heart attacks were more accurate than those who tried to evaluate every possible symptom.

But let’s go back to that fire captain. His gut feeling wasn’t magical. What had happened was that his brain, drawing on years of experience, had registered subtle cues: the heat didn’t feel right, the flames were the wrong color, the sounds were off. His conscious mind couldn’t name them, but his unconscious pieced them together and sounded the alarm. This kind of intuition, as Gary Klein documented in his book *Sources of Power*, is not a mysterious sixth sense—it’s pattern recognition built on thousands of tiny observations.

What does this mean for you? Imagine you’re faced with a hiring decision. You have three candidates, each with strong résumés. Your instinct says one of them is perfect. Should you spend hours comparing scores, references, and interview notes? Or should you trust that first impression? The research suggests that for simple decisions, conscious thought is fine. But for complex ones—the kind where you have too much information to process—you’re better off letting your unconscious do the heavy lifting. Spend a few minutes on the key facts, then get distracted. Do the dishes. Take a walk. Then decide.

The fire captain didn’t have time to deliberate. He made a snap decision that saved lives. Most of us have the luxury of time, but we misuse it. We overthink. We add complexity. And in doing so, we often make worse choices than if we had just gone with our gut—provided our gut has been trained by experience. The trick is not to stop thinking. It’s to know when to stop thinking, and let the brain do what it does best behind the scenes.

So the next time you face a tough decision, try something counterintuitive: don’t decide. Give it a moment. Let your unconscious earn its keep. You might be surprised at what you *just knew* all along.
