SUBJECT LINE: The Wrong Kind of Success is Holding You Back

Ed Catmull sat in a darkened screening room at Pixar in 1998, watching a disaster unfold. The film was *Toy Story 2*, and it was terrible. The story was a mess, the characters were flat, and the magic that had defined the first *Toy Story* was utterly absent. This wasn't just a rough cut; it was, by Catmull's own assessment, a potential studio-killing flop. The project had been chugging along for over a year, and the team felt they were succeeding—meeting deadlines, hitting milestones. But their "success" was built on avoiding the hard, ugly, iterative work of true creation. They had polished a fundamentally broken story.[^1]

We like to think that professional advancement is a straight line. You set a goal, you work hard, you avoid mistakes, and you succeed. Failure is the enemy, the resume stain, the story you have to explain away in an interview. It’s a comforting, meritocratic narrative. But there’s a problem with that theory: it treats career development like a video game where the objective is to never lose a life, rather than a craft where the objective is to learn how to build something worthwhile.

So what did Catmull and the Pixar team do? They threw out almost eighteen months of work. They scrapped the entire plot and started over from scratch with a brutal deadline looming. They embraced the colossal, expensive, public-facing failure of their current path. The resulting movie wasn't just good; it is often cited as one of the greatest sequels ever made. This seems like a dramatic, Hollywood exception. It turns out, it’s the rule for the most meaningful kinds of growth.

Let’s pause for a thought experiment. Imagine you’re hiring for a critical role. You have two final candidates. Candidate A has a flawless resume: top schools, seamless promotions, a track record of "meeting or exceeding" every goal. Candidate B has a more winding path: a startup that folded after three years, a project that was publicly cancelled, a noted "learning experience" from a previous boss. Who do you choose? Our instinct, conditioned by a culture that stigmatizes professional stumbles, is to choose A. But what if B’s resume doesn’t show failure—it shows *resilience*? What if the folded startup taught them more about leadership than a decade of smooth sailing ever could?

This is where the research gets interesting. The work of psychologist Carol Dweck on "fixed" and "growth" mindsets provides the scaffolding. A fixed mindset believes talent and intelligence are static. In this world, failure is a verdict—proof you don’t have "it." A growth mindset, however, believes abilities can be developed. Here, failure isn't a verdict; it's data. It's the necessary, uncomfortable feedback that tells you where to focus your effort. The careerist obsessed with an unblemished record is often trapped in a fixed mindset, opting only for safe bets that prove their innate talent rather than challenges that might expand it.[^2]

But mindset is one thing; tangible career outcomes are another. Consider the work on "grit" by psychologist Angela Duckworth. Her research famously found that grit—passion and perseverance for long-term goals—is a better predictor of success than IQ or talent. And what is the primary classroom for perseverance? It’s not the easy win. It’s the struggle. It’s the project that fails, the strategy that backfires, the criticism that stings. You cannot develop a callus without friction. A career devoid of calculated, instructive failures is like a body without an immune system—fragile and unprepared for the inevitable challenges of a complex world.

The corporate world, however, is often architected to scare us away from this kind of productive risk. Annual reviews reward flawless execution, not insightful experimentation. Managers are incentivized to hit quarterly targets, not to bet on uncertain, long-term innovation. This creates what some organizational psychologists call a "competence trap." We get very good at doing what we know works, and we avoid anything that might reveal a temporary lack of competence. We become experts in a shrinking world.

The most forward-thinking leaders and companies are now explicitly countering this. They are building what Catmull, after saving *Toy Story 2*, institutionalized at Pixar: a culture where "failure" is not a dirty word, but a recognized step in the process. They host "failure forums" or post-mortems without blame. They celebrate "intelligent failures"—those that happen in pursuit of a worthy goal, that are modest in scale, and most importantly, that generate learnings that are shared. The goal isn’t to fail for failure’s sake. It’s to create an environment where the fear of failure doesn’t strangle the possibility of a breakthrough.

Which brings us back to that screening room. Ed Catmull didn’t see a failed movie. He saw a team that had failed to engage in the difficult, collaborative, iterative process that makes Pixar, Pixar. The real success wasn’t the final, beloved film. The real success was the team’s willingness to stare into the abyss of their own disappointing work and choose the harder path. Their career success—and the studio’s—depended not on avoiding that moment, but on embracing it. The most valuable career capital you can build isn’t a list of victories. It’s a library of recoveries.

[^1]: The near-catastrophe of *Toy Story 2* is compounded by a now-legendary tech failure: an artist’s accidental command began deleting the film’s source files. They recovered most of it from a backup drive, but for a tense period, they thought years of work were vanishing from a server. Even the *rescue* was fraught with peril.
[^2]: This mindset shows up in subtle ways. A study on how students responded to challenging tests found that those with a fixed mindset were more likely to consider cheating after a poor result. Failure, to them, was an identity-threatening event to be obscured, not a problem to be solved.
