SUBJECT LINE: Why Winning Makes Champions More Terrified to Lose

Serena Williams stood at the baseline of Arthur Ashe Stadium, leading 5-1 in the third set of the 2015 US Open semifinal. She was one game from the final. She was the number one player in the world, the most dominant force her sport had ever seen. The stadium was roaring for her. And yet, as she would later confess, a single, paralyzing thought seized her: *What if I blow it?* She began missing shots she’d made since childhood. Her opponent, an unseeded underdog, won four straight games. Williams was suddenly on the brink of one of the most shocking upsets in tennis history. She eventually won, but the experience haunted her. “I got really nervous,” she said. “It’s like you get scared to win.”[^1]

We assume success is an inoculation against fear. The trophy, the promotion, the breakthrough—these are supposed to be armor, solidifying confidence and quieting doubt. The more you achieve, the less you should fear failure. That’s the comforting logic. But here’s the problem: that’s not how it works at all. For a startling number of people at the top of their game, success doesn’t quiet the inner critic; it gives it a megaphone. It creates a peculiar psychological trap where the prospect of failure becomes *more* terrifying, not less, precisely because you now have so much more to lose.

Psychologists have a name for a piece of this puzzle: impostor phenomenon. First described in 1978 by Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes, it’s that persistent, internalized fear of being exposed as a fraud despite evident success. It’s the award-winning scientist convinced her published work is a fluke, or the promoted executive certain the next project will reveal her incompetence. Research suggests it’s remarkably common among high achievers. The twist is that the phenomenon isn’t driven by a *lack* of accomplishment, but often flares *because* of it. Each new victory raises the stakes and amplifies the whispered question: *Can I do it again?* The success becomes a new benchmark you’re terrified of falling short of, a height from which the fall looks deadly.

To understand why, it helps to look back at a man who literally bet his life on a single, glorious success. Charles Goodyear spent years as a failed inventor, obsessed with stabilizing rubber. He was penniless, often pawning his family’s possessions to fund experiments. Then, in 1839, he had his legendary accident, dropping a mixture of rubber and sulfur on a hot stove and discovering vulcanization. He had it. The process that would make rubber durable, weatherproof, and revolutionary. He patented it, won medals, and was hailed as a genius. And yet, for the rest of his life, Goodyear was paralyzed. He couldn’t capitalize on his discovery. He spent decades in and out of debtors’ prison, embroiled in legal battles, unable to build an empire from his own world-changing invention. The biographer Samuel Kerkham’s assessment was blunt: Goodyear was “unfitted by nature for the management of financial affairs.” But one wonders if it was more than a lack of business acumen. Was he, perhaps, psychologically frozen by the sheer, unrepeatable perfection of his one miraculous discovery? Having reached the summit in a flash of accidental genius, every subsequent step was a potential descent.

This is the success paradox. The initial breakthrough—winning the first major tournament, landing the big client, launching the hit product—is often fueled by a kind of liberating desperation. You have nothing to lose. But that very success changes the psychological landscape entirely. Now, you have a reputation. You have expectations. You have a personal brand to uphold. The freedom of having nothing to lose is replaced by the burden of having something—often everything—to protect. Neuroscientists sometimes talk about the “loss aversion” baked into our brains; we feel the pain of loss more acutely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain. Success inflates the perceived size of what can be lost. Your past achievement becomes a mental possession, and the thought of losing it triggers a deeper, more primal alarm than the original desire to gain it ever did.

Think about it in your own life. Imagine you’re up for a promotion. The first time, you’re hungry. You pitch bold ideas, you take risks. Now imagine you get it. A year later, a bigger role opens up. This time, the calculus is different. You’re no longer the scrappy contender; you’re the established player. Would you pitch the same wild idea, or would you lean toward the safer, more proven path that protects the credibility you’ve already earned? The research on this is telling. Studies on “escalation of commitment” show that the more someone has invested in a certain identity or strategy—the more successful they’ve been with it—the harder it is to change course, even when change is needed. You become a prisoner of your own prior wins.

This paradox might also explain a curious finding in social psychology known as the Dunning-Kruger effect. The classic formulation is that incompetent people are too incompetent to know they’re incompetent, so they’re overconfident. The less often discussed flip side is that truly competent people tend to *underestimate* their abilities. Why? Because they possess enough knowledge to see the vast complexity of what they don’t know. Success, in a way, gives you the map to your own ignorance. The gold medalist knows in exquisite detail how a millisecond lapse or a subtle shift in the wind can lead to defeat. The novice has no such detailed map of disaster. The expert’s fear is informed; the novice’s confidence is blind.

So what do we do with this? The solution isn’t to avoid success, of course. It’s to recognize that the fear that comes with it isn’t a sign you don’t belong there. It might be the clearest sign that you do. It’s the proof that you understand the stakes. The goal is to manage the paradox, not to eliminate the feeling. Some of the most successful people learn to reframe the fear not as a threat, but as the necessary energy of high-stakes engagement—the price of admission to doing meaningful work.

Back on that tennis court, Serena Williams dug in. Facing match point against her, she didn’t play it safe. She went for a powerful, risky serve and followed it with a bold forehand winner. She fought through the fear that her own success had created and won the next two games, clinching the match. The terror of losing didn’t disappear. But she played through it. The paradox of success isn’t that it ends fear. It’s that it introduces a new, more sophisticated kind of fear. And winning, in the long run, isn’t about being fearless. It’s about knowing that the scary thought—*What if I blow it?*—is just the shadow cast by the very thing you’ve worked so hard to build.

[^1]: Williams discussed this match and her fear of winning in a revealing 2017 interview with *Vogue*, conducted as part of her iconic cover story. She described the mental spiral vividly, noting how the pressure of being so close to victory almost became a self-fulfilling prophecy.

[^2]: Goodyear’s story is a classic, tragic tale of the inventor who couldn’t invent a stable life for himself. His later years were a relentless series of lawsuits to defend his patent, and he died in 1860 with over $200,000 in debt—a massive sum at the time. The company that bears his name, Goodyear Tire and Rubber, was founded almost four decades after his death by Frank Seiberling, who named it in tribute to the inventor.
