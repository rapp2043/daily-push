SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Worst Decisions Are Written in Your Past

The CEO stood in front of his leadership team, holding a proposal for a new product line. The data was clear: market tests were weak, the development costs had ballooned, and his own advisors were skeptical. He scanned the faces in the room, thought for a long moment, and said, “We’ve come this far. Let’s double down.” The room went quiet. Two years and millions of dollars later, the project was quietly scrapped. Everyone who heard the story later asked the same question: What was he thinking? We like to believe our big decisions are rational, calculated affairs. We weigh the pros and cons, assess the current data, and choose the optimal path forward. It’s a comforting picture of human agency. But there’s a problem with that theory.

It turns out, the most powerful force in the decision-making room isn’t the spreadsheet in front of you. It’s the ghost of every decision you’ve already made. A wave of psychological research is revealing that our past actions don’t just inform our present choices—they actively hijack them. We aren’t navigating by the stars of logic, but by the deeply worn ruts of our own personal history. To understand why, we need to look at the three silent architects of our choices: action inertia, coherence justification, and the ghost in the machine we call the sunk cost fallacy.

The first architect is **action inertia**. Once we start moving in a direction, our psychology shifts from asking *“Is this right?”* to *“How do I keep going?”* Researchers studying this phenomenon have looked at settings where decisions should be purely procedural, like hospital admissions. In one often-cited examination of Dutch hospitals, it was found that physicians were significantly more likely to admit a patient to the cardiac ward if they had *already* been through the emergency room for chest pain, even if subsequent tests ruled out a heart issue. The initial action—the ER visit—created a momentum that was hard to stop. The decision was no longer about the patient’s current diagnosis, but about continuing the trajectory that was already in motion. It’s as if the brain, having committed to a path, develops a kind of intellectual loyalty to it, prioritizing consistency over recalibration.

But why would our brains be so loyal to a bad path? This brings us to the second architect: **coherence justification**. We have a profound, almost desperate need for our life’s story to make sense. Our past choices become the chapters of that story, and we will go to remarkable lengths to edit new information so it fits the existing plot. Psychologists have demonstrated this with a simple but powerful experiment called the “free choice paradigm.” A participant is asked to rate several items, like different art prints or CDs, then offered a choice between two they rated equally. After making the choice, they rate all the items again. Consistently, people will *increase* their rating of the chosen item and *decrease* their rating of the rejected one. The objective quality didn’t change. But the story did. The brain retroactively rewrites the value to make the past decision seem wise and inevitable. We don’t just choose; we become historians for our own lives, smoothing over contradictions to present a coherent narrative to ourselves.[^1]

Now, place these two architects together and you get the most famous, and most destructive, phantom in decision-making: the **sunk cost fallacy**. This is the ghost in the machine. It’s the voice that says, “I can’t quit now, look at all I’ve invested!” The investment isn’t just money; it’s time, emotion, and, most importantly, *identity*. The fallacy convinces us that continuing to pour resources into a failing venture will somehow redeem the lost ones. It’s coherence justification applied to a burning building: leaving would mean admitting the initial entry was a mistake, so we stay inside, searching for a reason that makes the fire part of the plan. Research in behavioral economics has shown this fallacy impacts everyone from individuals holding plummeting stocks to governments escalating military engagements. The past cost, which should be irrelevant to the future decision, becomes the only thing that matters because it has become part of our story.

So what do we do? The advice is simple and nearly impossible: learn to see the ghosts. Before your next big decision, pause and ask a peculiar question: *“If I were coming into this situation fresh today, with no history, what would I do?”* Strip away the narrative. Ignore the time spent, the money already spent, the personal pride invested. Look only at the current facts and the future paths. It is an act of mental time travel that requires divorcing your present self from your past self—seeing your own history as data, not destiny.

That CEO, staring at the failing proposal, wasn’t evaluating the product. He was defending the character in his story who had championed it for two years. He was trying to make the past make sense. The real decision wasn’t on the table in that meeting. It had been made, and quietly reinforced, a hundred times before. Our choices are rarely about the future we want to create. More often, they are about the past we are unwilling to leave behind.

[^1]: This need for narrative coherence might explain why we are so bad at predicting our future happiness. We imagine a linear story where getting the job or the house makes us happy, but we fail to account for how our storytelling brain will immediately absorb that event and demand a *new* source of coherence, setting the next goal. Happiness, in this light, isn't a destination but a byproduct of a story we find temporarily satisfying.
