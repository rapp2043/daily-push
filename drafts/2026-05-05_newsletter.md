SUBJECT LINE: The Law That Changed Everything—Before It Even Passed

In the summer of 2020, Tony West, Uber’s chief legal officer, sat in a conference room in San Francisco staring at a draft of a letter. It was addressed to the people of California, and its message was blunt: if voters didn’t pass Proposition 22—a ballot measure that would keep Uber’s drivers classified as independent contractors—the company would shut down its operations in the state. West didn’t send the letter. He didn’t have to. The threat alone, leaked to the press, did all the work.

And then something strange happened. Before a single ballot was cast, before the law had even been proposed—let alone passed—people started changing their behavior. Drivers in Los Angeles and San Diego began logging more hours, terrified that their income would vanish overnight. Riders started taking more trips, bracing for a future without Uber. The company itself saw a surge in activity, all because of a law that did not yet exist.

What are we to make of this? We tend to think that laws only matter once they are on the books. That is the story we tell ourselves: a bill is introduced, debated, passed, signed, and *then* it changes the world. But that is not quite right. The reality is that laws begin reshaping the world the moment they are proposed—sometimes even earlier. The mere possibility of a rule can send ripples through an entire system, producing outcomes that are unintended, ironic, and often invisible.

Consider the case of Texas, in the spring of 2021. State legislators were debating Senate Bill 8, a so-called “fetal heartbeat” bill that would ban abortion after six weeks—before many women even know they are pregnant. The bill had not yet been signed into law. It had not even passed both chambers. Yet, as Reason reported in April 2026, clinics across Texas began preemptively changing their policies. Some stopped offering abortions after six weeks, citing the threat of future lawsuits and the chilling effect of a law that might soon take effect. The law hadn’t passed, but it was already working. The mere introduction of the bill altered medical practice, limited access, and changed the choices available to women—all before a single governor’s signature.[^1]

Now, here is the irony. In California, the threat of Uber shutting down actually helped the company: drivers worked harder, rides became more available, and the public mood shifted in favor of Proposition 22. The threatened consequence—a shutdown—never materialized, but the fear of it produced exactly the kind of behavior Uber wanted: a more reliable supply of drivers. In Texas, the proposed abortion ban had the opposite effect: it accelerated the very restrictions it sought to impose. But in both cases, the pattern is the same. The law, as an idea, had more power than the law as a text.

We need to understand why this happens. It turns out there is a name for this phenomenon. Behavioral economists call it the “announcement effect.” When a policy is announced—even if it hasn’t taken effect—people adjust their behavior in anticipation. They do not wait for the law to land. They jump ahead, trying to protect themselves, maximize gain, or minimize loss. And those anticipatory moves can be bigger and faster than the law itself.

Take a moment and ask yourself: what would you do if you heard that starting next month, your job would be taxed at 90 percent? Would you wait for the new law to take effect? Of course not. You would start changing your spending, your savings, and your career plans immediately. That is the announcement effect in action.

But here is the twist: the announcement effect is not just a quirk of human psychology. It is a tool. Interest groups know that threatening a law can be as powerful as passing one. Lobbyists often leak proposed regulations precisely to force their opponents into preemptive concessions. Lawmakers themselves sometimes introduce bills they know will never pass, just to send a signal and shape behavior.[^2] The legislative process becomes a kind of psychological warfare.

What does this mean for us, the citizens? It means we need to pay attention not only to the laws that are on the books, but to the laws that are merely being discussed. The noise of politics—the press releases, the leaked memos, the rumors of what might come next—these are not just background chatter. They are the real machinery of change. The letter Tony West never sent changed more lives than any law he ever helped write.

So the next time you hear about a controversial bill that has just been introduced, ask yourself: what impact is it having right now, before it even has a chance to pass? Because the answer might surprise you. It might already be changing your world.

[^1]: This is not a new phenomenon. In 2012, before the Supreme Court ruled on the Affordable Care Act, several major hospital systems began implementing cost-cutting measures in anticipation of the law’s survival—only to roll them back when the Court struck down the individual mandate. The pattern repeats across decades.

[^2]: Consider the “fiscal cliff” negotiations of 2012. The mere possibility of automatic tax increases led to a wave of corporate stock buybacks and dividend payments in the last months of the year—a shift in behavior that actually reduced the government’s revenue in the short term.
