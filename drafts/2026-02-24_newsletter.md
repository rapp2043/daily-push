SUBJECT LINE: Why the Hospitals That Should Treat the Sickest Patients Don't

Lawrence Casalino spent decades watching American healthcare from the inside. As a physician turned health policy researcher at Weill Cornell Medicine, he observed a quiet revolution. Hospitals were buying up private doctor’s practices at a staggering rate, employing physicians directly. The rationale, repeated in boardrooms and policy papers, seemed unimpeachable: this kind of vertical integration would allow for better care coordination, especially for the most complex, costly patients—the elderly, the chronically ill, those on Medicare. It was a matter of aligning incentives and streamlining communication. By the time the COVID-19 pandemic hit, this model was so entrenched it was simply how things were done. But as Casalino watched the system strain under pressure, a nagging question formed. Was this elegant theory actually playing out on the ground? Or had the system engineered itself around a different, unspoken logic?[^1]

But here’s the problem with that theory. New research, the very study that caught Casalino’s eye, suggests the opposite might be true. The conventional wisdom is that hospital-employed physicians would naturally attract and manage a more complex, Medicare-heavy patient panel. It makes intuitive sense: you bring the doctors into the system to handle the system’s toughest cases. Yet the data tells a different story. According to this analysis, physicians after they were employed by hospitals actually saw a *smaller* share of Medicare patients compared to their peers who remained in independent practice. The integration meant to serve complexity seemed to be associated with serving less of it. This isn’t just a minor statistical blip; it’s a direct challenge to a foundational belief in modern healthcare management. Why would this happen? If the goal is to care for sicker populations, why would the structure designed to do that appear to be doing the reverse?

Let’s pause for a thought experiment. Imagine you run a hospital. You’ve just spent a significant amount of capital to acquire a physician practice. Your finance officers are looking at spreadsheets. What kind of patient is most profitable in our current system? It’s not the frail elderly Medicare patient with multiple chronic conditions. Their care is costly, and Medicare reimbursement rates are relatively fixed, often lower than private insurance. The financially attractive patient is the one with commercial insurance, which typically pays hospitals significantly more for the same services. The incentive, then, isn’t to funnel complexity inward. It might be to use your newly employed physicians as a referral network for profitable elective procedures and services—a hip replacement for a privately-insured 60-year-old, not the ongoing, resource-intensive management of a 80-year-old with heart failure and diabetes. The system’s financial gravity pulls toward commercial payers, not Medicare. This is the unspoken logic Casalino suspected.

This aligns with another uncomfortable strand of evidence: the growing pay gap between specialists and primary care physicians within these integrated systems. Primary care doctors are the ones who typically manage complex, multi-condition patients over time—the very work the integration was supposed to facilitate. Yet, as numerous analyses have shown, the income disparity between them and procedure-oriented specialists has widened, not narrowed, under hospital employment. The message in the compensation structure is subtle but clear. The reward lies in volume and procedures, often for less acutely ill patients, not in the time-intensive, cognitive labor of managing chronic Medicare cases. The system’s architecture, from its financial incentives to its employment contracts, whispers a priority that contradicts its public mission.

Then there’s the rise of Medicare Advantage. These are privately-run plans funded by Medicare. For a hospital, a patient enrolled in Medicare Advantage is often more financially predictable and sometimes more lucrative than one in traditional, fee-for-service Medicare. The research hints at this, too. The decline in traditional Medicare patients seen by employed physicians might be partially offset by an increase in Medicare Advantage patients. But this is a critical distinction. Medicare Advantage plans have networks and prior authorization rules. They actively manage care—and costs. A hospital employing physicians gains more control over steering these patients within its own network of services. So, the shift isn’t necessarily away from *older* patients, but away from the traditional Medicare model where care decisions are less controlled by the provider system. The integration may be less about managing medical complexity and more about managing financial risk and channeling patient flow.

So what are we left with? We built a system—hospital-employed physicians—on a theory of clinical efficiency for complex care. But the system operates inside a larger financial reality with its own set of rules. And in that reality, complexity is often a cost center, not a revenue driver. The result is a kind of institutional sleight of hand. A structure designed to handle complexity might actually be avoiding it, subtly optimizing for a different, more profitable kind of patient. This isn’t about malfeasance; it’s about the powerful, often invisible logic of incentives. When Lawrence Casalino looked at the integrated system during the pandemic, he wasn’t just seeing a care model under stress. He was seeing the consequence of this hidden logic, a system perfectly designed to get the results it was quietly being rewarded for, even if those weren't the results everyone said they wanted.
