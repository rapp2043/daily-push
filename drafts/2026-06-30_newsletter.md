SUBJECT LINE: The Hidden Cost of the Gene-Editing Revolution

In 2018, a team of scientists at the Wellcome Sanger Institute sat down to examine the results of an experiment they had run on mouse cells. They had used the gene-editing tool CRISPR to disable a single gene. It was a routine job—the kind of thing labs around the world were doing hundreds of times a week. But when the data came back, something was off. The cells had been edited, yes. But they had been edited far too much. Thousands of letters of DNA were missing, sometimes entire neighboring genes had been deleted. The scissors had not just snipped the intended spot; they had ripped out a whole section of the chromosome. And there was no good explanation for why.

This is the strange and unsettling truth about our most celebrated molecular tool. We like to think of CRISPR as a pair of precision scissors, a technology so elegant it will one day cure genetic diseases, create drought-resistant crops, and perhaps even rewrite our own inheritance. But as the Sanger team learned, the scissors are not always as precise as we imagine. They can cause what scientists call *unintended consequences*—large deletions, complex rearrangements, and even the loss of entire chromosomes. And the more we look for these side effects, the more we find them.

Take a moment and ask yourself: if you were a scientist about to try CRISPR on a patient, how confident would you be that only the target site was changed? Most of us would assume that the error rate is small—a few off-target cuts here and there. But what if the very act of cutting can set off a chain reaction inside the cell, a kind of molecular panic that rewrites not just one letter but whole paragraphs? That is the puzzle the Sanger team was left with.

The lead author of that 2018 study was Michael Kosicki, a geneticist then at the Sanger Institute. He and his colleagues had been using a standard CRISPR protocol to edit a gene in mouse embryonic stem cells. When they sequenced the modified cells, they found that in more than five percent of cases, the edit had produced a deletion of at least 250 base pairs. Some deletions were as long as several thousand base pairs. In a few cases, entire nearby genes had vanished. The mechanism, they later showed, was a breakdown of the cell’s normal DNA repair process. When CRISPR cuts both strands of DNA, the cell tries to stitch the broken ends back together. But sometimes, instead of neatly resealing the cut, it throws away a big chunk of the broken fragment. The result is not a precise edit—it’s a gaping hole.

That study was a wake-up call. But it was not the only one. In 2017, a controversial paper led by Dieter Egli at Columbia University showed that editing human embryos with CRISPR could lead to the loss of an entire chromosome. And in 2019, a group at Harvard found that CRISPR editing can cause large structural variants—inversions, duplications, and complex rearrangements—that standard sequencing methods often miss. The presence of these hidden changes means that many early CRISPR experiments, both in the lab and in early clinical trials, may have been built on shaky ground. If you don’t look for the big deletions, you won’t see them. And if you don’t see them, you can’t know whether your “successful” edit is truly clean.

Here is a thought experiment: imagine you are a farmer planting seeds. You treat the soil with a fertilizer that is supposed to boost a single nutrient. But what you don’t realize is that the fertilizer also leaches away a second nutrient and poisons the groundwater. That’s the situation we’ve been in with CRISPR. We were so excited about the ability to cut a specific gene that we overlooked the collateral damage.

Today, a new generation of scientists is trying to solve this problem. They are developing advanced versions of CRISPR that avoid creating double-strand breaks altogether, using methods like base editing and prime editing. These tools change one letter of DNA at a time without cutting the backbone, and early evidence suggests they cause far fewer large deletions.[^1] Other researchers are engineering Cas9 variants that nick only one strand instead of both, which seems to reduce the risk of catastrophic repair. And perhaps most importantly, sequencing technology is getting cheaper and more thorough, allowing labs to screen for unintended structural changes before moving an edited cell into a patient.

But here’s the catch: these safer methods are slower and more expensive. They require more design work, more validation, more patience. The very simplicity that made CRISPR so revolutionary—just cut and let the cell fix it—is also the source of its hidden messiness. As Kosicki put it in an interview, “We need to think about CRISPR not as a scalpel but as a chainsaw. With great power comes great responsibility, and also great cleanup.”[^2]

What the Sanger Institute team discovered in 2018 was not a flaw in CRISPR. It was a flaw in our understanding of what CRISPR actually does. They looked closely, and they saw the hidden cost of precision. And now that we know about it, we have to decide: are we willing to pay that cost, or are we going to design better tools? The answer will determine whether gene editing fulfills its promise—or leaves a trail of unintended consequences behind.

[^1]: The most widely used alternative, base editing, was developed by David Liu at the Broad Institute. It converts one DNA base into another, like changing a C to a T, without ever cutting the sugar-phosphate backbone. In a 2023 study, Liu’s team reported that base editing produced deletions in less than 0.1% of edits, compared to 5–10% for standard CRISPR. The difference is dramatic.

[^2]: A similar pattern of overconfidence followed the discovery of restriction enzymes in the 1970s. Early users assumed the enzymes cut only at their target sequence, only to learn that under certain conditions they could “star” and cut elsewhere. The lesson from molecular biology is always the same: every new tool comes with a hidden appendix of side effects.
