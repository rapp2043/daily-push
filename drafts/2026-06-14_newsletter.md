SUBJECT LINE: The Strange Thing Trees Do When Nobody Is Watching

In 2015, a young researcher named Thomas Crowther was finishing his PhD at Yale when he found himself overwhelmed by a question that seemed almost too simple to ask: How many trees are there on Earth? It was the kind of question that sounds like it should have been answered centuries ago. After all, we have mapped the ocean floor and sent probes to Mars. Surely someone has counted the trees. But when Crowther and his colleagues began looking, they discovered that the existing estimates were wildly inconsistent. They couldn't even agree on an order of magnitude. Some said four hundred billion trees. Others claimed closer to a trillion. So Crowther, now leading a team at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Zurich, decided to get the number right. He and his collaborators spent years combing through forest inventories, satellite data, and land-use records from around the world. When the results were published in *Nature* in 2015, the number was staggering: there are roughly three trillion trees on Earth. That is seven times more than the previous best estimate. It was a discovery that made headlines everywhere. But what nobody talked about at the time was the deeper question Crowther’s work inadvertently raised. If there are three trillion trees, and we know that each one absorbs carbon dioxide, then shouldn’t we be able to calculate exactly how much carbon is being stored? And shouldn’t that calculation be relatively straightforward? It turns out, it isn’t. That number, it seems, depends on something far stranger than we ever imagined.

You have probably heard the simple version of how trees store carbon. It goes like this: a tree pulls carbon dioxide out of the air through its leaves, uses sunlight to convert it into sugars, and then shoves most of that carbon into its trunk and branches in the form of wood. The more it grows, the more wood it makes, the more carbon it sequesters. It is a tidy, intuitive story. But here is the problem. A team of researchers led by Lucas Cernusak at James Cook University in Australia recently published a study in *Nature Communications* that upends this neat picture. What they found is that the relationship between photosynthesis and carbon storage is not nearly as direct as we assumed. A tree can keep photosynthesizing long after it has stopped adding wood to its trunk. And in a stunning violation of our basic expectations, that surplus photosynthetic activity does not always translate into stored carbon. In fact, under certain conditions—during a drought, for instance, or in very old trees—the tree might simply *use* that carbon for immediate respiration, burning it off rather than storing it. The carbon goes into the air, not into the wood. And then something strange happened. When the researchers ran the numbers on a global scale, they realized that our current models of carbon storage may be wildly overestimating the capacity of forests to absorb emissions. The very assumption that has underpinned billions of dollars in climate policy—that planting trees is a silver bullet—turns out to be built on surprisingly shaky ground.

Let me take you back to a forest in the Jura Mountains of Switzerland, in the early 1970s. A young Swiss forester named Ernst Zürcher is standing in a stand of beech trees. Zürcher is an oddity in the forestry world. He is fascinated not by the amount of wood in the trees, but by something almost occult: the way the pattern of tree rings seems to correspond to the phases of the moon. His colleagues think he is wasting his time. But over the next three decades, Zürcher will compile one of the most remarkable datasets in plant physiology. He will show that the rate at which trees add wood—the thickness of each annual ring—is closely tied to the lunar cycle, with the most growth occurring during the waxing moon. It is a finding that is deeply, almost absurdly counterintuitive. How could the moon, two hundred and forty thousand miles away, influence the amount of carbon a tree stores? The answer Zürcher proposes is delightfully indirect. He argues that the water table—the depth of groundwater—is affected by the moon's gravity, just like the tides. Higher water tables during a full moon make more water available to the tree's root system, and the tree can then use that water to support more photosynthetic activity. But here is the crucial twist: the carbon that is fixed during those periods of high growth does not all stay in the trunk. Some of it—depending on the health of the soil, the presence of mycorrhizal fungi, and even the tree's age—leaks out into the surrounding ecosystem. The tree, in other words, is porous. It is not a closed system. It is constantly exchanging carbon with the soil, with the air, and with the network of fungi that connects every tree in the forest.[^1]

[^1]: The extent of this exchange is still being debated, but it is not trivial. Some estimates suggest that up to ten percent of the carbon fixed by a tree is immediately transferred to the soil through its roots, where it may be stored for centuries—or quickly released by micro-organisms. It depends on soil temperature, moisture, and the species of micro-organisms present. In other words, the forest floor is a kind of carbon bank, and the rules of that bank are still being written.

So what does all this mean for our understanding of forests and climate? It means we have been treating a forest like a factory in which every tree is a machine that converts sunlight into stored carbon with perfect efficiency. But a tree is not a machine. It is a living organism that is part of a larger, infinitely more complex system. And that system has its own rules. One of the most startling implications of Cernusak's research is that the global capacity for forests to store carbon may be far more dynamic than we thought. Under one scenario that they modeled, if the planet warms by just two degrees Celsius, the amount of carbon stored in forests could drop by more than twenty percent—not because forests are dying, but because the trees are changing how they allocate their carbon. They will grow less wood and respire more carbon. And we will not even see it happening. The forest will look just as green, just as lush, just as alive as before. But beneath the canopy, the carbon arithmetic will have shifted.

Let me give you a thought experiment. Imagine you are standing in a grove of eucalyptus trees in the Blue Mountains of Australia. The year is 2026. You see a tree that is two hundred years old. It looks healthy. It has a massive trunk, a full crown of leaves, and it is doing what trees do: absorbing carbon dioxide from the air. Now ask yourself: Is this tree a net carbon sink? You might be tempted to say yes. But according to a long-term study conducted at the TERN ecosystem observatory in Australia, the answer is not so clear. Researchers there have been measuring the carbon flux of a eucalyptus forest for more than a decade. They found that the oldest trees—those over one hundred and fifty years old—were actually releasing more carbon than they absorbed. Not because they were dying. They were still photosynthesizing. But their respiration, which increases with age, had overtaken their photosynthetic output. They had become net carbon sources. The forest as a whole, however, was still a net sink, because younger trees, growing faster, were more than making up for the losses. But here is the catch: if the old-growth trees continue to age, and if younger trees are not replacing them at a sufficient rate—if there is a disturbance like a fire or a drought—the entire forest could flip from a sink to a source. And this is not a hypothetical. It is happening right now in parts of the Amazon, where a combination of deforestation and climate change has caused eastern Amazonian forests to switch from net carbon sinks to net carbon sources in recent decades.[^2]

[^2]: The Amazon story is particularly instructive. A study led by Luciana Gatti of Brazil's National Institute for Space Research, published in *Nature* in 2021, used aircraft to measure carbon flux over the Amazon basin. The southeastern part of the forest, which has been heavily deforested and is subject to prolonged dry seasons, now emits more carbon than it absorbs. The forest is not dying in a dramatic way. It is just breathing differently.

Why does this matter? Because of an assumption that is central to virtually every climate model currently in use. When policymakers and scientists talk about afforestation and reforestation as a solution to climate change, they implicitly assume that a tree planted today will absorb a specific amount of carbon over its lifetime. They model that absorption as a simple linear function: one tree, one unit of carbon per year, accumulating for decades. But the research from Cernusak, from Zürcher, from the TERN observatory, and from the Amazon tells us that the reality is much more nuanced. A tree planted in a drought-prone region might never store much carbon at all. A forest that looks healthy from above might be a net carbon emitter. And the moon, for goodness sake, might influence how much wood a tree adds to its trunk.

That brings us back to Thomas Crowther, the young researcher who wanted to count every tree on Earth. In the years since his 2015 *Nature* paper, Crowther has become one of the most influential figures in global forest ecology. He leads a massive project called the Crowther Lab at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology, which aims to model the entire planet's ecosystem as a single living system.
