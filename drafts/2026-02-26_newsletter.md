SUBJECT LINE: The Two-Word Change That Fixes Tenure

In the mid-1990s, a psychologist named Cynthia Fisher decided to read a few hundred letters of recommendation. Not for a job, but for a puzzle. These were tenure files for faculty in chemistry and biochemistry, and Fisher, along with her colleagues, was looking for patterns in the language. She read and coded, looking for the subtle adjectives and faint praise that might predict a candidate’s fate. What she found was a quiet, linguistic signal. Letters for female candidates, the data suggested, were more likely to include phrases like “she is pleasant” or “her teaching is adequate.” For men, the language leaned toward “brilliant” and “groundbreaking.” It was a whisper of bias, the kind so woven into the fabric of professional judgment that no one sending the letter—or reading it—might even hear it.[^1]

We like to think that the pinnacle of academic achievement, the granting of tenure, is a temple of pure meritocracy. It’s a years-long ordeal of peer review, publication counts, and committee deliberations, all designed to filter out prejudice and identify the best minds. It is a system built to be objective. But there’s a problem with that theory. Study after study has shown that racial and gender disparities persist in tenure rates at universities across the country. For all its procedural armor, the system seems to leak bias. The question is why. If everyone on a tenure committee is acting in good faith, and the data is all laid out on the table, how does inequity still find a way in?

It turns out, the answer might have less to do with what we decide and everything to do with *how* we decide. For decades, the push for equity in academia and beyond has focused on big levers: mandatory bias training, overhauling hiring committees, setting explicit diversity targets. These are the equivalent of rebuilding the engine. But what if the problem wasn’t the engine, but a single, sticky gear? A group of researchers at the University of Chicago’s Becker Friedman Institute began to wonder if a much smaller intervention—a nudge—could change the trajectory of an entire career.

The concept of a “nudge” comes from the work of economists Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein. It’s the idea that by subtly altering the way choices are presented—the *choice architecture*—you can guide people toward better decisions without restricting their freedom. It’s putting the fruit at eye level in the cafeteria, or making retirement savings plans opt-out instead of opt-in. The power of a nudge is that it works with human nature, not against it. It acknowledges that we are busy, biased, and often on autopilot, and it lays down a better path for that autopilot to follow.

So, what would a nudge for a tenure committee look like? The researchers proposed something almost laughably simple. Before a committee met to review a candidate’s file, they would be given a short, one-page document. This document wouldn’t lecture them about bias. It wouldn’t shame them or demand quotas. Instead, it would ask them to agree on the criteria for tenure *before* they looked at any candidate’s name or demographic data. It would ask them to define what “excellence in research” or “impact on the field” meant for this specific case. It would, in essence, force the committee to build their own objective scale before they started weighing the people on it.

This is a radical departure from how these meetings usually go. Typically, a committee gathers with a stack of files. They discuss Candidate A, holistically: her strong letters, his teaching scores, the prestige of their journal placements. Then they move to Candidate B and do the same. The comparison is inherently human, but also inherently fluid. The definition of “excellence” can subtly shift from one file to the next, bending to accommodate our unconscious impressions of who *looks* like a star. The nudge stops that drift. It establishes the finish line before anyone starts running.

And then something strange happened. In the universities that adopted this pre-review criteria intervention, the results were immediate. Preliminary data from the study showed a significant increase in the likelihood that faculty from underrepresented racial and ethnic groups would receive tenure. The size of the effect was not a minor statistical blip; it represented a meaningful closing of the equity gap. All from a one-page form. It was like discovering that a persistent leak in a complex pipe system could be fixed not by replacing the pipes, but by gently tightening a single, forgotten valve.

This should feel familiar in another context. For years, the world of symphony orchestras was dominated by men. Auditions were held behind a curtain, literally. When orchestras adopted blind auditions in the 1970s and 80s, the number of women hired increased dramatically. The curtain was a physical nudge. It forced judges to evaluate only the music, not the musician. The tenure committee form is a procedural curtain. It forces evaluators to judge the work, not the worker—or at least, to define the standards of judgment before the worker’s identity can cloud them.

Here’s a question for you: If you were on a committee and presented with two files—one for a candidate from a well-known, prestigious lab with glittering letters, and another for a candidate from a less famous school with solid but less dazzling credentials—which one would you instinctively favor? Now, what if you had to write down, in advance, the *minimum* number of publications in top-tier journals required for tenure? And what if both candidates met that bar? The nudge doesn’t make the choice for you. It just ensures you’re using the same measuring stick for everyone.

This brings us back to Cynthia Fisher and those tenure letters from the 90s. The language bias she identified wasn’t necessarily malicious. It was the output of an autopilot mind, reaching for the nearest cultural stereotype when describing “brilliance” or “diligence.” The committees reading those letters were likely doing the same thing, assembling an impression of a candidate from clues that were themselves tinged with bias. The new research suggests you don’t need to re-educate every letter writer or committee member. You just need to change the order of operations. Define the goalposts first. Build the scale. *Then* read the letters.

The profound lesson here is not about academia. It’s about the architecture of fairness. We often attack inequality as if it’s a monster requiring a hero’s sword—a massive policy change, a huge investment, a moral reckoning. But sometimes, the monster is just a lock that we’ve been using the wrong key on. The right key can be surprisingly small. It can be two words: “Define first.” Decide what matters before you see who’s in the room. It turns out that one of the most powerful tools for racial equity isn’t a mandate from on high. It’s a single sheet of paper, asking us to do our best thinking before our worst biases kick in.

[^1]: This research, focusing on gendered language, was part of a broader and well-documented pattern in academia. The famous 1999 study published in *Nature* by Wennerås and Wold, which audited postdoctoral fellowship applications in Sweden, found that female applicants needed to be nearly 2.5 times more productive in terms of publication counts to receive the same competency score as male applicants. The bias in letters of recommendation is a persistent, measurable phenomenon that sets the stage for later decision-making bottlenecks.
