SUBJECT LINE: Why Your AI Will Need to Fail

The laboratory at Insilico Medicine in Hong Kong looks like what you’d expect: banks of powerful computers, whiteboards scribbled with chemical formulas, researchers staring intently at screens. But if you watch closely, you’ll see something peculiar. The team isn’t just celebrating a breakthrough. They’re meticulously cataloging a disappointment. In 2021, they used artificial intelligence to design a novel drug candidate for a rare lung disease in a record 18 months. The story made headlines. What didn’t was the preceding years—and the thousands of AI-generated molecular designs that were absolute, unequivocal failures. The head of the lab, Alex Zhavoronkov, didn’t hide these dead ends. He built systems to study them, to feed them back into the machine. The success was built on a mountain of carefully examined flops.

We like to think the point of advanced technology, especially AI, is to eliminate failure. It’s a comforting narrative. We deploy algorithms to make processes more efficient, predictions more accurate, and decisions more foolproof. The promise is a smooth, error-free ascent. But there’s a problem with that theory. It turns out, the most powerful AI systems aren’t those that never fail. They’re the ones that fail the most intelligently.

Consider the data. In traditional drug discovery, the failure rate is legendary—around 90% of candidates that enter clinical trials never make it to market. The process can take over a decade and cost billions. The introduction of AI was supposed to slash those numbers, to make the search for new medicines a precise, targeted strike. But researchers at places like Harvard’s Wyss Institute are finding something different. AI isn’t reducing the raw number of dead ends; it’s accelerating the pace at which you can hit them. You can now generate and test thousands of flawed hypotheses in the time it used to take to test a dozen. The metric of success is shifting from *avoiding failure* to *optimizing your failure rate*.

So here’s the central question: In an age of supposedly omniscient machines, why is failing well becoming more important than ever?

The answer lies in a subtle shift in what AI actually does. Early AI was often about automation—following predefined rules to complete a task. But the modern, generative AI that designs drugs, writes code, or creates strategies is fundamentally different. It’s a discovery engine. It explores possibilities. And exploration, by its very nature, requires venturing into the unknown, where missteps are not just possible but guaranteed. The AI doesn’t have intuition; it has probability. It makes a guess, tests it against a model of the world, and learns from the mismatch. That mismatch is failure. As Stanford professor Andrew Ng has often framed it, machine learning is less about writing code and more about “showing the algorithm a lot of data and having it learn from its mistakes.”

This turns our traditional organizational psychology on its head. For decades, the study of high-performing teams, championed by researchers like Harvard’s Amy Edmondson, has focused on “psychological safety”—the belief that you won’t be punished for making a mistake. The goal was to create a space where the *human* fear of failure was reduced, so people could innovate. But with AI, we’re dealing with a tool that has no psychology to safeguard. It doesn’t feel shame or fear. The bottleneck is no longer the machine’s fragile ego; it’s our own human tendency to see its stumbles as bugs rather than data.

Think of it this way. Imagine two companies using AI to develop a new product. Company A sets its AI on a path, and at the first sign of a weird or suboptimal result, the engineers intervene. They tweak the parameters, guide it back to the “sensible” path. Company B does the opposite. They give their AI a broad goal and a massive “failure budget.” They instruct it to generate ten thousand flawed designs, and they have a parallel system—a kind of digital forensic team—whose sole job is to autopsy each dead end to understand *why* it didn’t work. Which company do you think discovers the truly revolutionary, non-obvious solution? The second one, almost every time. They’ve institutionalized learning from failure at a scale and speed no human team could match.

This is the strange paradox of the AI era. The technology that seems to epitomize infallible logic is making the intelligent management of failure the most critical human skill. A RAND Corporation report on military AI simulations noted that the most valuable wargames weren’t the ones the AI won, but the ones where it came up with a bizarre, catastrophic loss. Those failures revealed vulnerabilities in strategy that human planners had never considered. The failure was the gift.

Which brings us back to the lab in Hong Kong. Insilico’s breakthrough wasn’t a flash of silicon genius. It was the product of a loop—a loop that required their AI to generate countless bad ideas, and required their human team to have the humility and curiosity to study the wreckage. They didn’t just build an AI to succeed. They built an entire system to honor, parse, and recycle failure. The AI provided the sheer computational horsepower to fail at a staggering rate. The humans provided the wisdom to ask what those failures meant.

We are entering a time where the winner won’t be the one with the perfect algorithm, but the one with the best system for learning from everything that goes wrong. The failures are no longer the cost of doing business. They are the feedstock for success.

[^1]: The scale is almost incomprehensible. To generate that one viable drug candidate, the AI likely screened millions of virtual molecules, the vast majority of which would be useless or toxic. Each "failure" refined the model's understanding of chemical space.
[^2]: This is where the common sci-fi fear of AI goes astray. We imagine a cold, perfect intelligence. But the most powerful AIs are deeply embedded in messy, iterative, trial-and-error loops. They are not oracles. They are the world’s most relentless students, and their primary teacher is their own mistakes.
