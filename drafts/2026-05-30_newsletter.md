**SUBJECT LINE:** The Anti-Smoking Ads That Are Making People Vape

In the fall of 2019, a research psychologist named Jessica Pepper was sitting in her office at RTI International in Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, staring at a spreadsheet that made no sense. Pepper had spent years studying the effects of graphic warning labels on cigarette packs—those gruesome images of diseased lungs, rotting teeth, and cancerous throats that the FDA had been trying to mandate for nearly a decade. The theory behind them was straightforward: show people the real consequences of smoking, and they will be repulsed into quitting or never starting. The data from a national survey she was analyzing, however, told a different story. Young adults who had seen those graphic labels were not just more likely to report negative feelings about cigarettes—they were also *more* likely to have tried vaping. The intervention designed to reduce one harmful behavior appeared to be nudging people toward another. It was a classic case of the law of unintended consequences, and as Pepper and her colleagues would later write in a 2019 paper published in *JAMA Network Open*, it raised an uncomfortable question: Could an anti-smoking campaign actually be serving as an advertisement for vaping?

We tend to think of public health messages as simple, linear tools. You broadcast a warning, and people adjust their behavior accordingly. But people are not computers receiving commands. They are storytellers, and they process information through a web of prior beliefs, social norms, and subtle cues. A graphic image of a blackened lung might make a young person think: *Cigarettes are terrifying.* But it might also prompt a second, unspoken thought: *If cigarettes are that bad, then vaping—which looks clean, tastes like fruit, and doesn’t produce that smell—must be the safer choice.* This is the paradox at the heart of Pepper’s finding. The same fear that drives a teenager away from smoking may push them straight into the arms of an alternative they perceive as harmless.

This is not an isolated effect. Over the last decade, researchers have documented at least three distinct ways in which well-intentioned health warnings can backfire. Each one reveals a different crack in the logic of the public health message, and together they form a pattern that feels a lot like a trap.

The first is what we might call the “Fear Reversal.” The idea is that when you make a behavior seem extremely dangerous, you create a vacuum for a safer-looking substitute. In 2015, a team of researchers at the University of California, San Francisco led by Dr. James Lightwood published a study in the *American Journal of Public Health* examining the impact of California’s graphic anti-smoking campaign on youth vaping rates. They found that exposure to the ads was associated with a 10 percent increase in the likelihood of experimentation with e-cigarettes among 12- to 17-year-olds. The ads had a clear message: smoking kills. But the unintended consequence was that vaping, which had not yet been linked to the same kinds of graphic warnings, seemed to fill the gap. The fear of one thing created the permission for another. It is a logic that feels almost childlike—if a thing is not shown to be horrifying, it must be fine—but it operates on a deep, emotional level.

[^1]: This is the same psychological mechanism that made “Just Say No” campaigns so ineffective in the 1980s. When kids were told to say no to drugs without being given a credible alternative, the prohibition itself made the proscribed behavior more intriguing. The absence of specific horror stories about marijuana, for example, inadvertently made it seem less dangerous than cocaine or heroin.

The second unintended consequence is what we might call the “Rebellion Effect.” This one is even trickier because it exploits the very emotional energy that anti-smoking ads are trying to generate. When you tell a teenager that cigarettes will rot their teeth and give them cancer, you are also, unintentionally, telling them that cigarettes are something that adults are terribly afraid of. And for a certain kind of adolescent, an adult’s fear is an invitation. The more graphic the warning, the more visceral the disgust, the more the act of smoking becomes a gesture of defiance. In a 2018 study published in *Tobacco Control*, researchers at the University of Bristol found that young people who reported the strongest emotional reactions to graphic warnings were *more* likely, not less, to express an intention to smoke. The researchers called it “reactance”—a psychological term for when a threatening message triggers the opposite response. You put a picture of a damaged lung on a pack of cigarettes, and the teenager thinks: *You can’t tell me what to do.* The warning becomes a dare.

But the most subtle and perhaps most dangerous unintended consequence is the “Information Paradox.” This one operates quietly, beneath the surface of conscious awareness. When you tell people that something is dangerous, you are also, necessarily, telling them that there is a spectrum of danger. Cigarettes are very dangerous. What about vaping? The ad didn’t say. So in the absence of a specific warning, the mind fills the gap with a favorable comparison. A 2020 study led by Dr. Andrea Villanti, then at the University of Vermont, published in *Nicotine & Tobacco Research* demonstrated this effect precisely. They asked a group of young adults to rate the harmfulness of various tobacco products. Those who had been exposed to graphic anti-smoking ads did not just rate cigarettes as more harmful—they rated e-cigarettes as *less* harmful than their peers who had not seen the ads. The same message that increased fear of one product decreased fear of another. It was like turning a dial. The more the darkness of smoking was emphasized, the lighter everything else seemed by comparison.

So what are we supposed to do with this? Public health campaigns are not going to stop warning people about the dangers of smoking. Nor should they. But the research forces us to ask a more uncomfortable question: Should we be warning people about vaping at the same time? Should the same graphic imagery that is reserved for cigarettes be applied to e-cigarettes? The answer, it turns out, is not simple. In 2021, the FDA began requiring e-cigarette packages to carry a warning about nicotine addiction, but they stopped short of the kind of graphic imagery used on cigarette packs. The reason, according to an analysis by the RAND Corporation, was that there was no evidence that such warnings would be effective—and some evidence that they could produce the same unintended consequences. You can’t warn people away from everything, because at a certain point the warnings cancel each other out, or worse, create a hierarchy of danger that leads people to the second-worst option.

Dr. Jessica Pepper, the psychologist in North Carolina, has thought about this a great deal. In the years since her 2019 paper, she has shifted her research focus toward understanding how young people *compare* risks across products. What she has found, in a series of studies conducted with colleagues at the FDA, is that the story is never just about one product. It is about the entire landscape of risk perception. When you put a warning on one thing, you are implicitly creating a signal about everything else. The human mind cannot help but draw comparisons. And in that game, the safest option is often the one that has never been painted in the most terrifying colors.

Think of it this way: a 16-year-old walks into a convenience store. On the counter, there is a pack of Marlboros embossed with a photo of a collapsed lung. Next to it, there is a sleek, metallic JUUL pod with a small warning label that says “contains nicotine.” Which one looks more dangerous? The answer is obvious. And that is the problem. The graphic warning, designed to save lives, has become a recommendation for the alternative. It’s not that the ad lied. It’s that it told only half the story. And in public health, as in life, half a story can be worse than no story at all.
