SUBJECT LINE: The Plan to Lower Drug Prices Could Kill the Next Cure

There is a moment that comes in every university technology transfer office, several times a year, that is so routine nobody bothers to write it down. A researcher walks in with a discovery—a molecule that seems to do something interesting in a petri dish. The tech transfer officer begins the dance: file a patent, find a company willing to take the risk, structure a licensing deal that gives the university a cut of future sales. It is a process that has become so ingrained over the past four decades that it feels like the natural order of things. But in early 2024, a group of those officers gathered for a conference and the mood was different. The Federal Register had just published a new guidance from the Biden administration, one that proposed to do something unprecedented: allow the government to seize patents from companies that developed drugs with federal funding—if the drug was priced too high. The mechanism is called “march-in rights,” and it had been written into the Bayh‑Dole Act of 1980 but never successfully used. Now the administration wanted to change that. And the technology transfer officers were terrified.

But before we understand why they were terrified, we need to understand the world they were trying to protect. That world was built by a piece of legislation that, at the time, seemed like a minor bureaucratic fix. Before Bayh‑Dole, any invention made with federal money belonged to the government. Universities had little incentive to commercialize their discoveries, and companies had little incentive to license them, because the government could always give the same technology to a competitor. The result was a graveyard of promising discoveries sitting on lab shelves. Bayh‑Dole flipped that logic. It gave universities the right to patent and exclusively license federally funded inventions. Overnight, the university became a partner in the innovation economy.

Consider the story of Northwestern University and a chemist named Richard Silverman. In the late 1980s, Silverman was studying a compound that seemed to calm overexcited neurons. He had no idea it would become one of the best‑selling drugs in history. But Northwestern filed a patent, licensed the compound to a small company called Parke‑Davis, and eventually to Pfizer. The drug, pregabalin, sold under the brand name Lyrica, went on to generate more than sixty billion dollars in worldwide sales. Northwestern has received hundreds of millions in royalties. And millions of patients with nerve pain and fibromyalgia got a drug that worked. That is the story Bayh‑Dole made possible. It is a story repeated, in variations, for drugs like Gardasil, Keytruda, and many others. The bargain was simple: the government provides the seed money, universities provide the discoveries, companies provide the billions of dollars and the decade of clinical trials needed to turn a molecule into a medicine. The company gets a patent. The public gets a new drug.[^1]

Now, the Biden administration’s new guidance on march‑in rights proposed to rewrite that contract. The idea sounds appealing: if a drug developed with taxpayer money is priced too high, the government can step in, seize the patent, and license it to a cheaper manufacturer. Lower prices for everyone. But here is the problem. The researchers and technology transfer experts who testified against the proposal—and dozens of them did—argued that the threat itself would poison the very ecosystem that produces those drugs. They pointed to the case of Norvir, a protease inhibitor used to treat HIV. In the early 2000s, the drugmaker Abbott raised the price of Norvir from about $1.50 to $8.50 per day. Patient groups petitioned the government to use march‑in rights. The government investigated and ultimately declined, arguing that the price increase alone did not mean the drug wasn’t available on reasonable terms. But the threat did not end there. It hung over the industry, a reminder that the government could, at any time, decide what “reasonable” means.

Here is the puzzle: why did the march‑in threat fail to lower prices in the Norvir case? And why do researchers fear that using it now will cause more harm than good? The answer lies in the nature of the partnership. Companies that license university inventions take enormous risks. The vast majority of drug candidates fail. The ones that succeed must earn enough to cover the many that fail. If a company knows that the government might step in and seize a successful drug’s patent because it is “too expensive,” it will either demand a much larger share of future profits up front, or it will simply refuse to license university discoveries altogether. That is not speculation. That is what technology transfer officers told the government in their public comments.
