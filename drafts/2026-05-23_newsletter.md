SUBJECT LINE: How a City Known for Pablo Escobar Became a Cultural Capital

In 2004, Sergio Fajardo sat in a cramped office in Medellín’s city hall, staring at a map of the city’s hillsides. The map was covered in red dots—each one a homicide. At that point, Medellín had one of the highest murder rates in the world, hovering around eighty murders per 100,000 people. But Fajardo wasn’t looking at a crime map. He was looking at a library map. He had a plan to build ten new public libraries in the poorest neighborhoods, each one designed by a world-class architect. Critics told him it was insane. Why spend millions on beautiful buildings in places where the state barely had a presence? Fajardo had an unusual answer. “We will build the most beautiful buildings in the most violent places,” he said. “Because beauty is a form of justice.”

It’s a line that sounds more like a TED Talk slogan than a serious policy. But Fajardo meant it literally. Over the next few years, Medellín invested heavily in what became known as “social urbanism”—using architecture, public transportation, and cultural spaces to heal a broken city. And something strange happened. The homicide rate dropped by more than 70%. The city began to be talked about not for its drug violence, but for its music festivals, its art museums, and its innovative urban design. In 2013, Medellín was named the Most Innovative City in the World by the Urban Land Institute, beating out New York and Tel Aviv. How did a city that was synonymous with Pablo Escobar become an unexpected capital of culture?

To understand that, we need to understand why conventional theory says it shouldn’t have worked. The standard model of cultural renaissance comes from people like Richard Florida, the urban theorist who argued that cities attract the “creative class” by having lots of coffee shops, art galleries, and a tolerant vibe. In that view, culture is a byproduct of affluence and educated populations. You get a Starbucks first, then a museum. But Medellín had none of that. It was poor, violent, and isolated. It was exactly the kind of place that, according to the theory, should never be a cultural capital.

And yet it worked. Why?

The answer, it turns out, lies in a principle that city planner Jaime Lerner once called “urban acupuncture.” Lerner, the former mayor of Curitiba, Brazil, believed that you could transform a city not by building huge infrastructure projects everywhere, but by applying small, precise interventions at key points—like an acupuncturist inserting a needle. A library in a violent neighborhood, a cable car line connecting a slum to the city center, a public square where there was once a dirt field—these aren’t just amenities. They are signals. They tell the people living there: you matter. The city sees you. And when people start to feel that, something shifts in the social fabric.[^1]

Let’s pause for a moment and ask you a question. Imagine you are the mayor of a city with a murder rate like Medellín’s. You have limited budget and enormous crime. What do you do? Most people answer: hire more police, build more prisons, crack down on gangs. That’s what we assume works. But Fajardo’s team asked a different question: what if the violence is not caused by a lack of policing, but by a lack of hope? What if the best way to reduce crime is to give people something to belong to?

Here is where the data gets interesting. In the early 2000s, researchers at the University of the Andes in Bogotá began studying the impact of Medellín’s library parks. They found that, in the neighborhoods where new libraries opened, children’s test scores improved. More importantly, the number of teenagers who reported feeling “part of the city” doubled. The libraries weren’t just book repositories. They became community centers, offering free classes, concert spaces, and places where parents felt safe sending their kids after school. A study published in the Journal of Urban Affairs in 2016 confirmed a significant correlation between the presence of these cultural infrastructure projects and the reduction in homicide rates in nearby neighborhoods. Not causation, the authors cautioned, but a strong, statistically significant association.

That’s the part that still surprises urban planners. The conventional wisdom is that culture follows safety. You need low crime before you can have open-air concerts and sidewalk cafés. But Medellín flipped the sequence. It used culture to create safety. And that is a reversal that has implications far beyond Colombia.

Consider the case of the Metrocable. This is a cable car system originally built to connect the hillside shantytowns to the valley floor. Most transit experts thought it was a tourist gimmick. But an analysis by the Inter-American Development Bank showed that the Metrocable not only cut commute times by an average of forty minutes per day—it also reduced social isolation. Residents of the hillside neighborhoods, known as comunas, began to visit the city center more frequently. They started using the libraries. They signed up for workshops in music and painting. One woman, interviewed in 2010 by The New York Times, said: “Before the cable car, I felt like I was living in another country. Now I feel like I am part of Medellín.”

That feeling of belonging—that’s the needle. Lerner called it “urban acupuncture,” and there is a growing body of research that supports it. Psychologists have long known that social connectedness is one of the strongest predictors of resilience and reduced violence. When you give people a beautiful, dignified public space, you are essentially telling them the social contract is real. The city owes them something, and they owe the city something in return. That is how you turn a narco-state into a cultural capital.

But let’s be honest. The transformation is not perfect. Medellín still has poverty, and crime has crept back up in recent years. Culture alone cannot solve structural inequality. The city’s art museums and music festivals—like the Festival Internacional de Tango, which draws thousands of visitors—are genuine achievements, but they exist alongside persistent segregation. The hillside neighborhoods are still poorer than the valley. The difference now is that the city has a story about itself that is not just about Escobar. It has a different narrative, and narratives matter.

In 2014, the band (a local group, not the Rolling Stones, but a smaller community orchestra) performed in one of the new library parks. Hundreds of people came. Children danced. An older man, a former gang member, told a reporter from El Colombiano: “I never thought I’d see my grandchildren playing violins in this neighborhood.” That moment—that single, improbable moment—is what the theory of urban acupuncture is all about. You don’t need to fix everything at once. You just need to create a few places where the future looks different, and let the future spread.

So what is the lesson for cities everywhere? It is that cultural capital is not a natural resource discovered only in historic European capitals or booming tech hubs. It is something that can be built, deliberately and patiently, wherever you stick a needle of beauty into the skin of a forgotten place. The cause of culture is not pre-existing wealth. The cause of culture is the belief that culture is possible. And sometimes, the most effective way to create that belief is to build something beautiful in a place where beauty has been absent for a long time.

We started with Sergio Fajardo and his library map. In 2023, Medellín was named one of the World’s Best Cities by Condé Nast Traveler—not for its safety, but for its culture. The same hillsides that were once covered in red dots are now covered in street art, murals of the Virgin Mary and of peace symbols. The libraries are full. And the cable cars carry tourists and locals together, suspended over the city that refused to accept its fate. It turns out that the most unexpected capital of culture was the one that had the least reason to believe in itself—and believed anyway.

[^1]: Urban acupuncture is a real concept, popularized by architect Jaime Lerner. He used it in Curitiba in the 1970s, installing small parks and bus shelters that transformed the city. It is not a metaphor; it is a documented planning approach.

[^2]: One of the most famous of Medellín’s library parks is the Parque Biblioteca España, designed by architect Giancarlo Mazzanti. It sits in the hillside neighborhood of Santo Domingo, and its black, white, and brown cubes are visible from much of the city. It has become a symbol of the city’s turnaround.

[^3]: A 2018 report by the World Bank on Medellín’s urban transformation highlighted that the city’s homicide rate fell from 84 per 100,000 in 2002 to 24 in 2016. Not all of that can be attributed to libraries, but the simultaneous investment in social infrastructure is widely cited as a contributing factor.
