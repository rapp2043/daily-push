SUBJECT LINE: The Unintended Consequences of Loving Chimpanzees Too Much

In July 1960, a twenty-six-year-old woman named Jane Goodall sat on a ridge in Gombe Stream National Park, watching a male chimpanzee she would later call David Greybeard. He was doing something no scientist had ever documented: he was using a blade of grass to fish termites from a mound. Goodall held her breath. She had been waiting for months, enduring the skeptical warnings of her mentor Louis Leakey, who told her the chimpanzees would never accept her. But now, in this one moment, everything changed. She sent a telegram to Leakey that read, “Have found something extraordinary.” He famously responded, “Now we must redefine man, redefine tool, or accept chimpanzees as human.”

That telegram is one of the most famous in the history of science. It launched a revolution in how we think about animals. Goodall’s work—naming her subjects, living among them for decades, documenting their individual personalities and emotional lives—shattered the wall that Western science had erected between humans and the rest of the animal kingdom. It was, by any measure, a triumph.

But there’s a problem with that story. It leaves out something important. Something that Goodall herself would later struggle to understand. Because the same methods that produced those incredible insights also produced a cascade of unintended consequences—consequences that, for a time, threatened the very chimpanzees she had devoted her life to protecting.

Here’s the puzzle: how could one of the most compassionate approaches to animal research end up causing harm? The answer lies in the details of how Goodall operated, and in a deeper truth about the relationship between observation and interference.

When Goodall first arrived at Gombe, the chimpanzees were terrified of humans. They would flee at the sight of her. To win their trust, she began leaving bananas. It started small—a few fruits placed near her camp. But soon she escalated to provisioning: regular feedings designed to bring the chimps close enough for detailed observation. It was a technique used by other primatologists at the time, and it worked brilliantly. Within months, the chimps began to approach her. Before long, they were stealing bananas from her tent. Goodall had gained access to a world no one had ever seen.

What came next was extraordinary. She watched chimps use tools, hunt cooperatively, wage war. She recorded their greetings, their reconciliations, their capacity for what she called “compassion.” She gave them names—Fifi, Flo, Mr. Worzle—treating them not as numbered subjects but as individuals, which was heresy to her peers. “This is the story of a family,” she wrote in her memoir, and she meant it.

But here’s the thing about bananas. They are not neutral. By provisioning the chimps, Goodall inadvertently changed their behavior in ways that would take decades to fully understand. The bananas created competition. Dominant males began to monopolize the feeding station, which intensified aggression. In one documented case, a female named Passion and her daughter Pom hijacked the feeding station and killed the infants of other females, a level of violence rarely seen before. Goodall herself described the “four-year war” between two chimpanzee groups at Gombe—a conflict that left several males dead—and while she believed this was natural, later research suggested that the provisioning may have exacerbated the tensions.

And then there were the diseases. Chimpanzees and humans share more than 98 percent of their DNA. That means they are vulnerable to many of the same pathogens. The researchers and tourists who came to Gombe brought with them germs to which the chimps had no immunity. In 1966, a polio outbreak swept through the park. Goodall watched in horror as her beloved chimps lost the use of their limbs, one by one. Some died. Others, like the old male Mr. McGregor, survived but were left paralyzed. The polio virus almost certainly came from humans—either from the local human population or from researchers themselves. Goodall later acknowledged this possibility: “We did not know what we were doing,” she wrote.

It wasn’t just disease. The very closeness that made Goodall’s research so powerful also made the chimpanzees habituated. They lost their fear of humans—a natural survival instinct. This became a major problem when conservationists later tried to protect them. Habituated chimps are easier to poach. They are more likely to raid farms. They can become aggressive toward tourists. Decades after Goodall’s early work, researchers at Gombe are still grappling with the legacy of that habituation.

Let me ask you something. Imagine you are a scientist in the 1960s, determined to understand an animal that has never been studied up close. You need them to come to you. So you offer a few bananas. It seems harmless. But what if that one small decision has a range of ripples you cannot see? What if it creates competition, disease, dependency—and all of this happens while you are busy making history? That is the story of Goodall’s unintended experiment.

It would be easy to cast this as a cautionary tale about scientific arrogance. But that would miss the point. Goodall did what she did out of love, not heedlessness. And she was not alone; nearly all early primatologists used provisioning. The real lesson is more unsettling: even our best intentions, when channeled through the lens of observation, can have effects we cannot predict. There is no neutral observer. The act of watching changes the thing being watched.

By the 1990s, Goodall had become a global activist for chimpanzee conservation. She publicly acknowledged the mistakes of her early methods. The banana provisioning stopped. Researchers at Gombe now maintain strict health protocols—masks, quarantines, distance. Goodall herself has said, “I look back on those early days with a mixture of joy and sorrow.” Joy for the discoveries; sorrow for the price paid.

And so we return to that first morning on the ridge, Goodall watching David Greybeard. It was a moment of pure wonder. But the story did not end there. It continued—through the polio, the wars, the lessons learned. And in the end, Goodall’s legacy is not just the revolution she sparked, but the humility she gained. She showed us that understanding a creature does not mean controlling it. Sometimes, the greatest act of love is to step back.

[^1]: The use of provisioning in primatology was common until the 1970s. Today, most field sites have abandoned it, but the ethical debate continues. In *Chimpanzee Politics*, Frans de Waal describes how even minimal provisioning can distort social hierarchies.

[^2]: Goodall’s own account of the polio epidemic appears in her book *Through a Window*. She describes watching the chimps lose their ability to move, and her decision to inject them with an early experimental polio vaccine. It was a desperate act, and it saved some lives—but also further reinforced the chimps’ dependence on humans.

[^3]: The “four-year war” at Gombe, from 1974 to 1978, was documented by Goodall and later by Richard Wrangham. While intergroup aggression occurs in wild chimpanzees, the density of the Gombe population—inflated by provisioning—may have intensified the conflict.
