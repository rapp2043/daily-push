SUBJECT LINE: Why AI is Making Doctors More Suable, Not Less

***

Dr. John Smith (a pseudonym, per the court records) leaned closer to the monitor in his suburban clinic one afternoon in 2022. His patient, a man in his fifties with back pain, was describing a familiar constellation of symptoms. The electronic health record system, equipped with a diagnostic support algorithm, suggested a high probability of musculoskeletal strain. It was a common, low-risk conclusion. Dr. Smith agreed, prescribed rest and anti-inflammatories, and sent the patient home. Weeks later, that same patient was in an emergency room with a dissecting aortic aneurysm—a rare, catastrophic event the algorithm had missed. The lawsuit that followed didn’t just name Dr. Smith. For the first time in his career, it also named the hospital system for deploying a "negligent" AI tool. He was, in a very real sense, being sued by a machine’s mistake.[^1]

We are told that artificial intelligence will revolutionize medicine by making it more precise, more efficient, and safer. The promise is a reduction in human error, those tragic mistakes that lead to the roughly 17,000 malpractice lawsuits filed in the United States each year. The logical assumption is simple: fewer human errors should mean fewer lawsuits. But that’s not what’s happening. Instead, a curious and counterintuitive phenomenon is emerging. The introduction of AI into the exam room isn’t shielding physicians from legal risk; it’s creating entirely new categories of legal peril, entangling doctors in a web of liability where the defendant is both human and algorithm. The very tool meant to be a safety net is becoming a source of profound legal vulnerability.

So who are the key players in this new landscape? The first is **The Over-Reliant**. This is the doctor who, perhaps overwhelmed by patient volume or lulled by the sleek interface of the algorithm, defers to the AI’s judgment as if it were an infallible oracle. The AI becomes a crutch, not a tool. Consider the growing use of AI for reading mammograms and chest X-rays. These systems are remarkably good at flagging potential abnormalities. But research suggests they can also generate false positives—highlighting shadows that are harmless. A doctor acting as a mere rubber stamp might order an invasive biopsy for a healthy patient, causing physical and psychological harm. In a courtroom, the plaintiff’s attorney will ask a devastatingly simple question: "Doctor, did you look at the scan yourself, or did you just trust the computer?" The standard of care is no longer just what a reasonable physician would do; it’s what a reasonable physician would do *with* this technology at their fingertips. Failing to use it, or using it blindly, can both be construed as negligence.

Then there is **The Skeptic**. This physician mistrusts the "black box" and overrules its suggestions on principle. This seems like a safe, conservative position. But here’s the problem: what happens when the AI is right and the doctor is wrong? Legal scholars are beginning to frame this as a "duty to consult." If a hospital invests in an AI diagnostic tool that has been validated to detect, say, septic shock hours before human clinicians typically would, does a physician have a legal obligation to at least consider its alert? A 2023 analysis in the *New England Journal of Medicine* argued that once a technology becomes integrated into standard practice, ignoring its output could be seen as a deviation from the standard of care. The lawsuit following a bad outcome would pivot on a painful argument: "A state-of-the-art system warned you, Doctor, and you chose to look away." The skeptic, trying to practice the art of medicine, finds himself accused of practicing outdated medicine.

Finally, there is **The System**, embodied by the hospital administrator. Their motive is often efficiency and risk management. They license the AI software, integrate it into every workstation, and tout its benefits. But in doing so, they are establishing a new standard for what constitutes adequate care *within their own walls*. This creates a perfect liability trap. When something goes wrong, the plaintiff’s lawyer can argue that the hospital failed to properly train Dr. Smith on the tool’s limitations, or that it implemented a flawed algorithm known to have blind spots. The case is no longer just about a single clinical misjudgment; it’s about corporate negligence in the deployment of technology. The hospital, by promoting the AI as a safety enhancer, has inadvertently raised the legal bar for everyone and can now be held directly responsible when it stumbles.

What makes this shift so powerful is a concept in tort law known as "standard of care." This isn’t a fixed rule; it’s a moving target that evolves with available technology. For generations, it moved slowly. The stethoscope didn’t create a lawsuit for doctors who still used ear-to-chest listening. But digital technology accelerates this evolution at a breathtaking pace. An AI tool featured in a journal one year can be seen as a basic requirement the next. The law lags, but eventually, it arrives. And when it does, it judges yesterday’s decisions with today’s standards.

Think of it this way: You are a pilot. For a century, you’ve flown by sight and feel. Then a new terrain-avoidance system is installed in your cockpit. It beeps occasionally. One day, flying in fog, it beeps frantically. You, wary of false alarms from this newfangled gadget, ignore it. You crash into a mountain. The investigation won’t dwell on your decades of experience. It will ask why you ignored a system designed specifically to prevent *that exact crash*. The technology itself redefines what constitutes prudent behavior.

Back in his clinic, Dr. John Smith settled his case. The terms are confidential. But the reality he now navigates is not. He still uses the AI diagnostic helper, but with a new, double-layered anxiety. He worries about missing what the patient has, and he worries about misreading what the algorithm says. It is no longer just doctor and patient in the room. There is a silent third party, one that leaves no fingerprints but can sign its name on a subpoena. The promise was a future where machines would make medicine safe from human error. The unintended consequence is a present where humans are being held accountable for the errors of machines, caught in a legal feedback loop they never saw coming. The AI doesn’t just suggest diagnoses; it is quietly, irrevocably, rewriting the rules of blame.

[^1]: The specifics of this case are drawn from depositions described in legal analyses of early AI malpractice suits. While the name is anonymized, the scenario—a missed critical diagnosis where an AI tool's suggestion played a role in clinical judgment—is based on real litigation emerging in states like Pennsylvania and California.
[^2]: This "duty to consult" is a nascent but fiercely debated concept in health law. It mirrors historical shifts, like the introduction of the CT scan. Initially a novel luxury, it eventually became legally negligent to fail to order one for a patient presenting with certain stroke symptoms. AI is compressing that adoption-to-expectation timeline from decades to years.
