**SUBJECT LINE:** The Fish That Forgot to Metamorphose

Let me tell you about a moment in the history of science that has the potential to upend everything we thought we knew about how life crawled out of the water.

In the summer of 2021, a team of paleontologists sat in a dusty lab at the University of Chicago, staring at a laser scan of a fossil that had been buried in the Canadian Arctic for nearly 375 million years. The rock was unprepossessing—a gray slab no bigger than a dinner plate. But the creature inside it was something else. It had a shoulder blade shaped like a tetrapod’s, and a hip joint that could have supported weight on land. It had fins that looked like they were halfway to becoming legs. And yet, as the researchers rotated the virtual model, they noticed something peculiar. The bones of the fin were blocky and flattened, not slender like a fish’s. They were built for pushing against something solid—perhaps a riverbed, or a muddy bank. But this was not a tetrapod. This was a fish. And not just any fish: it was a close relative of *Tiktaalik*, the iconic “fishapod” that had long been hailed as the missing link between water and land. This new animal, described in a 2022 paper in *Nature* by Thomas Stewart and his colleagues, was named *Qikiqtania wakei*. And it told a story that was far stranger than the one we had been telling.

For decades, the standard narrative of vertebrate land invasion went something like this: a group of fish—the elpistostegalians—began to evolve limbs, lungs, and a neck. They ventured onto land, and over millions of years they became the ancestors of all amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals. It was a neat, linear story, with a clear hero: the fish that left the water. The frog’s metamorphosis—from a water-breathing tadpole to a land-hopping adult—was often held up as a microcosm of that grand evolutionary journey. It made intuitive sense. First you’re aquatic, then you change. Simple.

But here’s the problem with that story: it’s not quite right. Evolution, it turns out, does not work like a frog. It works more like a bush—a tangled, messy bush in which some branches give up on land, others never quite commit, and still others try something completely different. The fossil record of the Devonian period, roughly 380 to 360 million years ago, is full of these experiments. And the more we find, the less the frog-metamorphosis model fits.

Let’s start with the poster child of the early tetrapod story: *Tiktaalik roseae*. Discovered in 2004 on Ellesmere Island, *Tiktaalik* was a fish with a flat head, a mobile neck, and fins that contained the rudiments of a wrist and fingers. It was clearly a creature that could prop itself up on its fins and perhaps take a few clumsy steps in shallow water. For a long time, it was considered the perfect “missing link”—a single, pivotal step on the path to land. But even *Tiktaalik* raises a puzzle. Its fins were not built like the sturdy legs of early tetrapods such as *Acanthostega* or *Ichthyostega*. They were still fins—just modified. So what exactly was *Tiktaalik* doing with them? Neil Shubin, the paleontologist who led the team that found it, has suggested that *Tiktaalik* was a kind of “walking fish” that used its fins to navigate the bottom of shallow streams and estuaries. It wasn’t trying to leave the water—it was just getting better at living in it.

Then we have *Qikiqtania*. This fish, which lived roughly at the same time as *Tiktaalik*, tells an even more surprising story. Its fin bones were robust, but its elbow joint was shaped in a way that would have made it impossible for the fish to actually support its weight on land. The joint was a hinge that only allowed forward-and-backward motion, not the kind of rotation needed for walking. In other words, *Qikiqtania* was a fish that had the hardware for land—the shoulder blade, the thick fin rays—but had abandoned the effort. It had turned back. It chose to remain in the water. Stewart and his colleagues called it a “reversal”: a secondarily aquatic fish that lost its ability to crawl. Evolution, it seems, can also go in reverse.

But wait—there’s a third category. Consider *Acanthostega gunnari*, discovered in Greenland in the 1980s by the late Jennifer Clack. *Acanthostega* was a true tetrapod, with eight fully formed digits on each limb. And yet, as Clack painstakingly showed, its limbs were so weak that it could not have walked on land. They were paddles, not legs. *Acanthostega* didn’t live on land; it lived in weedy shallows, using its many digits to navigate through dense vegetation. It had lungs, but it also had functional gills. It was an aquatic tetrapod—a fish with legs. It took the next eighty million years for tetrapods like *Ichthyostega* to develop the sturdy ribcages and stronger limbs needed for true terrestrial locomotion. And even then, *Ichthyostega* walked in a peculiar, seal-like sculling motion, not the four-footed gait we imagine.[^1]

So what does this all mean? Let me ask you a question. When you picture the first fish that crawled out of the water—the legendary “first step on land”—what do you see? A single heroic fish, maybe a little like a mudskipper, hauling itself onto a beach and giving birth to the entire terrestrial vertebrate lineage. That image is wrong. Instead, imagine a committee of fish, each with its own plan. Some evolved limbs and then re-evolved fins. Some evolved digits but never left the water. Some limped onto land only to be pulled back by the tide of natural selection. The real story of land invasion is not one of progress toward a single goal; it is a story of parallel experimentation, of many different solutions to the same set of problems. The frog’s metamorphosis—a tidy transformation from one state to another—is a beautiful piece of biology, but it is a terrible metaphor for evolution.

Here is where we get to the deeper lesson, and it’s one that extends far beyond paleontology. We humans love a linear narrative. We love the idea that change proceeds in an orderly, step-by-step fashion. That’s why the frog-metamorphosis analogy was so appealing. But the fossil record of the Devonian tells us something more profound: that the most successful innovations often come from a process of messy diversification, not from a single straight line. The early tetrapod lineage did not succeed because it had one perfect adaptation. It succeeded because it had many imperfect adaptations, all being tested at once. Some failed, some succeeded, and some succeeded for a while before being abandoned. The system as a whole—the “bush” rather than the “ladder”—was what produced the resilience to survive the mass extinctions and environmental upheavals of the next 350 million years.[^2]

So let’s return to the fossil slab from the Canadian Arctic. The gray rock holds the bones of a fish that tried to walk, then changed its mind. It is not a failure. It is evidence that evolution is not a ladder from simple to complex, or from water to land. It is a garden of forking paths, where every path—even the ones that lead nowhere—has its own logic and beauty. Next time you see a frog leaping across a lawn, remember that its ancestors did not simply climb out of the water once and never look back. They went in and out, back and forth, forward and backward. And we are all the richer for it.

[^1]: For an excellent account of Jennifer Clack’s work, see her book *Gaining Ground: The Origin and Evolution of Tetrapods*. Her discovery of *Acanthostega* fundamentally rewrote the timeline of land adaptation.
[^2]: This insight—that messy diversification trumps linear progress—echoes a broader principle in organizational theory called “requisite variety.” The biologist W. Ross Ashby first articulated it in the 1950s: the more varied an organism’s repertoire of responses, the better it can survive unpredictable changes. The Devonian fish were masters of requisite variety long before anyone named it.
