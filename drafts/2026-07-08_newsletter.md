SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Doctor Might Be Wrong About Parkinson’s and Exercise

In 2003, Dr. Jay Alberts, a neuroscientist at the Cleveland Clinic, went on a 450-mile bike ride across Iowa with a woman named Cathy. Cathy had Parkinson’s disease. They rode a tandem bike—Alberts in front, Cathy in back. At the end of each day, Alberts noticed something startling. Cathy’s tremors were quieter. Her movement was smoother. By the time they reached the finish line, her handwriting had improved. It was as if the disease had taken a step backward. Alberts was a researcher. He knew the textbook: Parkinson’s is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder. You can manage symptoms with medication, but you cannot slow down the underlying damage. That was the received wisdom. But what he saw on that bike ride made him wonder: what if the textbook was wrong?

It turns out that for decades, the medical establishment assumed that exercise was merely palliative—helpful for mood and muscle tone, but irrelevant to disease progression. The logic seemed sound: Parkinson’s destroys dopamine-producing neurons, and no amount of movement can replace those cells. But here’s the problem with that logic. The brain is not a passive organ. It adapts. It rewires. That capacity—neuroplasticity—is exactly what exercise appears to exploit. And the evidence, once you start looking, is astonishing.

Let’s begin with the kind of exercise that matters most. In 2018, a landmark study known as SPARX, published in *JAMA Neurology*, took patients with early-stage Parkinson’s and put them on a regimen of high-intensity treadmill walking—four times a week, for six months, at a heart rate that pushed them to what felt like a nine out of ten on the exertion scale. A control group did nothing. The results? The high-intensity group stopped getting worse. Their motor scores on the standard UPDRS scale held steady, while the control group declined. For the first time, a clinical trial showed that exercise could alter the trajectory of the disease. Not just improve symptoms, but slow the underlying progression.[^1]

[^1]: This is not an isolated finding. A 2023 meta-analysis in *Neurology* pooled results from over a dozen trials and concluded that higher intensity exercise consistently reduced motor decline. The effect was small, but real.

But not all exercise is created equal. Consider resistance training. A 2015 study led by Dr. Margaret Schenkman at the University of Colorado found that patients who did resistance exercises three times a week for two years maintained scores on the UPDRS, while a group that did stretching and balance exercises alone saw steady deterioration. The key, it seems, is *loading* the muscles and joints in a way that forces the brain to recruit and coordinate motor units. “Exercise is like a drug,” Alberts later told *The New York Times*. “We need to figure out the right dose, frequency, and type for each patient.”

Then there is the most surprising category: complex, skill-based movement. Studies on boxing-for-Parkinson’s programs, pioneered by nonprofits like Rock Steady Boxing, show that the rapid, unpredictable demands of punching a bag and dodging jabs trigger a unique kind of neuroplasticity. A 2021 study from Purdue University reported that after six months of boxing training, patients showed improved balance, gait, and even cognitive processing speed. The mechanism appears to be that novel, challenging movements stimulate the release of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, a protein that acts as a fertilizer for neurons. The more complex the task, the more BDNF your brain produces.

This is where the real insight lives. For years, neurologists told patients: “Exercise is good for you, but it won’t change the course of your disease.” That advice feels reasonable, even kind. But it subtly discourages patients from pushing themselves. If they knew that high-intensity, resistance, or complex motor training could actually buy them years of independence, would they work harder?

Here is a thought experiment. Imagine yourself as a Parkinson’s patient, newly diagnosed. Your doctor hands you a prescription for carbidopa-levodopa, the standard medication. Then she adds: “And do whatever you can to stay active.” Those words—“whatever you can”—are an invitation to take it easy. But what if she said: “You need to train three times a week at 80% of your maximum heart rate. It’s not optional. It’s as important as your medication.” How would you change your behavior?

That is exactly what the new research suggests we should be saying. A 2024 study from Northwestern University, presented at the American Academy of Neurology annual meeting, followed 210 patients over two years. Those who engaged in high-intensity aerobic exercise at least 150 minutes per week showed a 30% slower progression in motor symptoms. The findings held even when controlling for medication use and disease duration. “We need to stop thinking of exercise as complementary,” said lead researcher Dr. Daniel Corcos. “It is therapeutic.”

Back to that 2003 bike ride. Alberts went on to run a clinical trial on forced cycling, where Parkinson’s patients ride a stationary bike at a cadence slightly faster than they can manage on their own. The result: a pattern of brain activation that mirrors the effect of deep brain stimulation. The brain, it turns out, can be tricked into healing itself. The patient, Cathy, remained active long after that ride. “I felt like I had control again,” she told a reporter. Control. That’s the word that matters.

For decades, the question was: can exercise slow Parkinson’s progression? The answer is yes—and the reason is as simple as it is profound. The brain is not a machine that runs down inexorably. It is a garden that responds to the seeds you plant. And the best seeds, it turns out, are the ones that require you to sweat.

[^2]: The flip side is that not all patients can tolerate high-intensity exercise, especially as the disease progresses. That’s why researchers are now exploring lower-intensity alternatives, such as rhythmic auditory stimulation—walking to a metronome beat—which can also improve gait and reduce freezing episodes. The principle remains: specificity matters more than intensity alone.

[^3]: The belief that Parkinson’s progression is unstoppable dates back to James Parkinson’s 1817 essay, which described a “shaking palsy” that inevitably worsens. It took nearly two centuries to realize that the brain’s plasticity could slow that decline. Sometimes the oldest assumptions are the most stubborn—and the most wrong.

[^4]: One patient in the SPARX trial described the difference this way: “Before the study, I felt like I was on a escalator going down. After six months, I was still on the escalator, but it had stopped. I could stand still and look around.” Metaphor can be as powerful as data.
