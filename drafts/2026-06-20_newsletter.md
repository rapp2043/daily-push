SUBJECT LINE: The Biology of Your Next Decision

In the late 1990s, a young neuroscientist named John Coates was sitting in a cramped office at the University of Cambridge, staring at a spreadsheet that would upend everything we thought we knew about financial markets. Coates had spent years on Wall Street as a derivatives trader, and he had noticed something peculiar: the traders who made the most money weren’t the ones with the best analytical skills. They were the ones with a certain kind of emotional and physiological edge. So he decided to run a study. He collected saliva samples from a group of traders on the floor of the London Stock Exchange, measuring their levels of cortisol and testosterone before, during, and after trading sessions. What he found was startling. On days when a trader’s testosterone was high, he made more money, taking bigger risks with greater success. But when cortisol surged—the stress hormone—traders became risk-averse and made losses. The market, Coates realized, was not a purely cognitive arena. It was a biological one.  

We like to think that our economic decisions—whether to buy a stock, take a job, or choose a health insurance plan—are the products of careful deliberation. Economists have built entire models on the assumption that we are rational actors, weighing costs and benefits with cold precision. But there’s a problem with that theory. It turns out that before your brain can compute a cost-benefit analysis, your body has already cast a vote. The biology of choice—the hormones flowing through your blood, the wiring of your prefrontal cortex, the ancient emotional circuits that evolved long before the first market—shapes every decision you make. And the most powerful evidence for this idea comes not from a laboratory, but from the tragic story of a man who lost the ability to feel.  

In the 1980s, a patient known in the scientific literature as Elliot walked into the office of neurologist Antonio Damasio at the University of Iowa. Elliot had been a successful businessman, a good husband, a reliable father. Then a small tumor was removed from the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region deep in the front of his brain. The surgery was a success. His IQ remained in the superior range. His memory was intact. He could solve complex logic problems with ease. But something had changed. Elliot could no longer make decisions.  

He would spend hours deliberating over trivial choices—what pen to use, where to go for lunch—and even the most consequential decisions, such as where to invest his life savings, became paralyzing. Without the ability to feel emotion, Elliot had no internal compass to guide him. Damasio’s insight, which he later called the somatic marker hypothesis, was that emotions are not the enemy of rational choice. They are its foundation. The physiological signals that arise from our bodies—a racing heart, a knot in the stomach, a sense of unease—serve as markers that narrow the field of options, allowing us to act. Without those markers, we are lost in an infinite sea of possibilities.  

Consider another experiment, conducted more than two decades later by the neuroscientist Alan Sanfey at the University of Arizona. Sanfey asked volunteers to play the Ultimatum Game, a classic economic test where one player proposes how to split a sum of money and the other can accept or reject the offer. To an economist, any positive offer should be accepted—it’s free money. But people routinely reject offers that feel unfair, even when it costs them. While volunteers played inside an fMRI scanner, Sanfey observed that when an unfair offer was made, a region called the anterior insula lit up—the same area responsible for feelings of disgust. The brain was saying: this is repulsive. The decision to reject was not a calculation. It was a visceral response. And then something strange happened. People who had damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex—the same region Elliot had lost—accepted every unfair offer. They felt no disgust, so they saw no reason to punish.[^1]  

The implication is radical. The rational economic model assumes that we start from a blank slate, that we compute utility and proceed accordingly. But biology provides the architecture. The hormones that surge during adolescence—testosterone, estrogen, oxytocin—fundamentally shift the calculus of risk and reward. The chronic stress that reshapes the hippocampus alters how we weigh long-term consequences. The dopamine reward system, evolved to seek food and mates, now drives us to check our phones or chase a stock tip.[^2]  

[^1]: Damasio’s work on Elliot is documented in his book *Descartes’ Error* (1994). An updated discussion of the somatic marker hypothesis can be found in Damasio’s later work, *The Strange Order of Things* (2018).  

[^2]: Sanfey’s Ultimatum Game study was published in *Science* in 2003: “The Neural Basis of Economic Decision-Making in the Ultimatum Game.”  

But here is where the story gets even more interesting. If biology shapes choice, then the best decision-makers are not those who suppress their biology, but those who understand it. Coates, after his years on Wall Street and in the lab, now argues that the most successful traders are the ones who have learned to read their own physiological signals—who know when their cortisol is spiking and can step away, or when their testosterone is dangerously high and can resist the temptation to double down. He calls this “biological awareness,” and it is a skill that can be trained.  

Let me ask you a question. Imagine you are a trader. The market is volatile. Your heart is pounding. Your palms are sweating. Do you buy or sell? If you are like most people, you will try to think your way through it. But think about what is happening inside your body. The racing heart is triggering a cascade of stress hormones that narrow your focus. The sweat means your sympathetic nervous system is on high alert. Under those conditions, your brain is not capable of cool deliberation. It is primed to flee or freeze. The best decision, paradoxically, might be to do nothing—to recognize that your biology is not an enemy to overcome, but a signal to listen to.  

We end up back where we started, with John Coates and his saliva samples. Today, a growing number of behavioral economists are integrating biological markers into their models. They are studying how hunger affects judicial rulings, how sleep deprivation alters CEO risk-taking, how the menstrual cycle shifts consumer preferences. The field of “neuroeconomics” is no longer a fringe curiosity. It is becoming central to our understanding of choice.  

What does this mean for you, the everyday decision-maker? It means that the next time you make a choice—large or small—ask not just what you think, but what your body is telling you. That knot in your stomach might be the most rational part of you. That rush of excitement might be leading you astray. The biology that shapes your choices is not a flaw. It is the very machinery that makes choice possible.
