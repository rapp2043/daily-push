SUBJECT LINE: The Wrong Kind of Failure

James Dyson stood in his backyard shed in England, ankle-deep in dust and frustration. It was 1979, and for the fifth year running, his attempt to build a better vacuum cleaner had resulted in another pile of failed prototypes. Each version—a tangle of cardboard, duct tape, and makeshift cyclones—would clog or lose suction. His neighbors, watching the strange man haul bag after bag of dirt into the shed for testing, thought he was mad. Dyson would later claim he built 5,127 prototypes before he got it right. That number, 5,127, has since become a modern parable. We repeat it in boardrooms and commencement speeches as proof of a cherished idea: that failure is not the opposite of success, but its essential fertilizer. We tell each other that to innovate, you must fail early and fail often. That each misstep is a stepping stone. It’s a comforting story, one that turns the pain of setbacks into an investment. But there’s a problem with that theory.

It turns out, Dyson’s story is the exception, not the rule. And we have misunderstood what kind of failure his story actually represents.

In the summer of 2024, a team of researchers from Northwestern University’s Kellogg School of Management published a study that quietly upended decades of motivational poster wisdom. They wanted to know: when an individual fails, how likely are they to eventually succeed? They examined data from three very different arenas: scientists applying for grants, entrepreneurs launching ventures, and people looking for employment after a terrorist attack. The pattern they found was startlingly consistent. For the vast majority who failed initially, success did not follow. Failure was most often a dead end, not a detour. Grant applicants who were narrowly rejected on their first try were not more likely to win later; they were slightly *less* likely. Entrepreneurs whose first ventures collapsed were not statistically more likely to succeed on their second or third attempt. The data painted a clear, if uncomfortable, picture: in the real world, success rarely follows failure.

This presents a puzzle. If failure is such a poor predictor of future success, why does the legend of the gritty, persistent underdog feel so true? Why do we cling to the Dysons and the Edisons—the heroes who failed their way to glory?

Part of the answer lies in a trick of memory. We remember the one who triumphed after 5,127 attempts. We forget the thousands of other inventors who also built 5,127 failed prototypes and then, on attempt 5,128, built another failed prototype, until they ran out of money, hope, or both, and disappeared from history entirely. This is survivorship bias in its purest form. We see the winners and craft a narrative around their path, ignoring the invisible graveyard of losers who walked the exact same road. The story of failure-as-lesson depends entirely on the ending. A lesson that leads nowhere is just a mistake.

But the Northwestern researchers suggested something more subtle than mere bias. The critical factor, they argued, wasn't *whether* you failed, but *how* you failed, and what you could learn from it. In the grant application data, they noticed a revealing detail. Applicants who received detailed, critical feedback on their initial rejection *did* show improved chances on subsequent tries. The failure itself was useless; it was the specific, actionable intelligence gathered from the failure that had value. A silent, unexplained failure taught nothing. It was just a stop sign.

This brings us to Thomas Edison, the other patron saint of productive failure. “I have not failed 10,000 times,” he is famously misquoted as saying. “I’ve successfully found 10,000 ways that will not work.” The quote is apocryphal, but the sentiment is telling.[^1] It reframes blind alleys as a deliberate process of elimination. Yet historians of innovation note that Edison’s real genius wasn’t just persistence; it was his maniacal focus on creating laboratory conditions where failures yielded clear, diagnostic information. When a light bulb filament burned out, he didn’t just sigh and try another material at random. He would create a vacuum so pure, and measure the burn time so precisely, that the failure told him exactly why that particular carbonized bamboo thread or platinum wire was inadequate. His failures were experiments. Most of our failures are just accidents.

So let’s play a game. Think of the last time you failed at something meaningful—a project that flopped, a proposal that was rejected. Now ask yourself: did that failure come with a detailed autopsy report? Did you receive a spreadsheet of data explaining precisely which lever you pulled that caused the mechanism to jam? Or did it just end with a vague sense of disappointment and a closed door? For most of us, it’s the latter. We experience what the researcher and writer Paul Graham calls “default failure”—the kind that happens when you run out of resources, not when you’ve conclusively answered a question. Default failure teaches you one thing: that you failed. It contains no secret map to a different route.

This is the hidden flaw in our “fail fast” mantra. Silicon Valley champions the idea, but it often confuses speed for learning. You can fail fast and still learn nothing, if you’re not engineered to capture the signal from the noise. A startup that burns through venture capital and shuts down after two years may have “failed fast,” but if the founders didn’t isolate *why* their customer acquisition model was fatally flawed, they are no wiser than when they began. They have merely spent more money to arrive at the same dead end. Their failure was not the productive, Edisonian kind. It was the silent, Northwestern-data kind.

And this is where we must reconsider James Dyson in his shed. We focus on the staggering number of failures, but we miss his process. Each of those 5,127 prototypes was a question. Why did the cyclone clog on particle *this* size? What happened to airflow when the chamber was tapered *that* way? With each collapsed cardboard model, he wasn’t just failing; he was conducting a ruthless interrogation of physics. The failure was not the lesson; the *measurement* of the failure was. He created an environment where every wrong answer narrowed the possibilities for the right one. Most of us, when we fail, are just throwing mud at a wall. Dyson was running a controlled experiment.

The unsettling truth of the Northwestern study is not that perseverance is futile. It’s that raw, undifferentiated failure is overrated. Grit only matters when it is paired with a feedback loop so precise that each setback eliminates a world of possibilities. The successful aren’t those who fail the most. They are the ones who, when they fail, have built a system to understand exactly why.

So the next time you hear a story about legendary persistence, listen carefully. The hero isn’t celebrating failure. They are celebrating a very specific, information-rich, and often expensive quality-control process. They are celebrating a diagnostic. The rest of us, when we stumble, are usually just falling down. There’s a world of difference between the two. And that difference determines whether you’re on your way to building a billion-dollar company, or just sweeping another pile of dust out of your shed.
