SUBJECT LINE: Why Irrationality Is Actually a Kind of Logic

It was the early 1970s, and two psychologists at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem were puzzling over a curious pattern. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky had been asking people a simple question about a disease outbreak. Imagine, they said, that six hundred people are about to die. You have two treatment options. Option A saves two hundred people for sure. Option B gives a one-third chance that all six hundred will be saved and a two-thirds chance that nobody will be saved. When they put it that way, a strong majority—something like 72 percent—chose Option A. The certain saving of two hundred lives felt right.

Then they asked a different group the same scenario, but with the options framed differently. Option A now meant that four hundred people would *die* for sure. Option B gave a one-third chance that nobody would die and a two-thirds chance that all six hundred would die. Faced with that framing, the same kind of majority—about 78 percent—chose Option B. The logic of the two problems is identical. The only difference is whether you focus on what is saved versus what is lost. Kahneman and Tversky had stumbled onto something strange: human beings do not have a fixed scale for what is rational. We have a scale that shifts with the frame.

This is the puzzle that launched an entire field of study. Behavioral economics is often presented as the study of why we are irrational. But that is not quite right. The more interesting truth—the one that Kahneman and Tversky spent decades uncovering—is that our irrationality follows predictable rules. It has a logic all its own. And once you understand that logic, you can begin to see that many of the choices we make, the ones that seem foolish or shortsighted, are actually the product of a mind that is doing something rather clever, even if it is not what the economists of the 1950s expected.

Here is the problem with classical economics. The model of *Homo economicus*—the rational actor who always knows what he wants and always calculates the optimal path—is a beautiful piece of theory. It gave us supply curves and demand curves and efficient markets. But it never described how real people behave. As Richard Thaler, the University of Chicago economist who later won a Nobel Prize for his work in behavioral economics, once put it: “We need to stop thinking of people as Econs and start thinking of them as Humans.” Humans, unlike the Econs, forget things. They get emotional. They are swayed by the way a choice is presented. They hate losing more than they love winning. That last fact—loss aversion—is one of the most robust findings in all of behavioral science. In one classic experiment, Thaler and his colleagues gave half the students in a class a coffee mug emblazoned with the university logo. The mugs were ordinary, worth about six dollars. Then they asked the students who got the mugs how much they would be willing to sell them for. The median asking price was around five to seven dollars. Then they asked the students who had not gotten a mug how much they would pay to buy one. The median offer was about two to three dollars. The gap—the endowment effect—is not a mistake. It is a reflection of the fact that what we own instantly becomes more valuable to us than what we do not own. Loss aversion is not irrational; it is a deep psychological truth about the way we attach ourselves to things.

So what does behavioral economics actually teach us? Not that we are bad decision-makers, but that we are good decision-makers *within a particular context*—the context of a world that has evolved to reward quick, efficient judgments. The problem is that the modern world—with its mortgages, retirement accounts, and complicated insurance plans—demands a different kind of thinking. It asks us to be the rational actor when our brains are wired to be the hunter-gatherer.

Take the famous marshmallow test. In the 1960s, Walter Mischel at Stanford put preschoolers in a room with a single marshmallow and told them: if you can wait fifteen minutes, you will get a second marshmallow. Some kids waited. Some ate the marshmallow immediately. Later, those who waited tended to have better life outcomes—higher SAT scores, lower body mass. The standard interpretation: willpower is a virtue. But the behavioral economist sees something different. The kids who ate the marshmallow were not necessarily irrational. They were responding to a different set of incentives. When you are three years old, fifteen minutes is an eternity. The future is abstract; the present is concrete. The mind is not failing to be rational—it is prioritizing the immediate, which is what evolution trained it to do. The mistake is in assuming that the “rational” choice is always the one that maximizes long-term gain.

Here is a thought experiment. Imagine you are a doctor, and you have to decide whether to perform a surgery that has a 95 percent survival rate. Most doctors would say yes. Now imagine the same surgery has a 5 percent mortality rate. Suddenly, it feels riskier—even though the numbers are identical. That is framing. It is not a failure of intelligence; it is a feature of how our brains process potential threats. The emotional system, which evolved to keep us safe from saber-toothed tigers, does not do percentages. It does feelings. And that feeling of “5 percent will die” activates a different part of the brain than “95 percent will live.”

The real insight of behavioral economics is not that we are flawed. It is that we are *predictably* flawed. That predictability matters because it allows us to design systems that work *with* human nature rather than against it. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein called this “nudge” theory. A nudge is a small change in the environment that makes the better choice easier to make—without taking away anyone’s freedom. You want people to save more for retirement? Instead of asking them to opt in to a 401(k), make them opt *out*. The automatic enrollment nudge dramatically increased savings rates in the United States, because it played to our tendency toward inertia. Inertia looks like laziness, but it is actually a form of logic: the default is the path of least resistance, and for most of us, most of the time, that path is the right one.

The agenda of behavioral economics, then, is not to fix human beings. It is to understand the hidden logic of human choice. The field has been criticized for being “old news” or for simply cataloguing biases. But its greatest contribution is subtler. It reminds us that the model of the perfect rational actor is not a useful benchmark. We are not imperfect versions of that model. We are something else entirely. We are beings who think in stories, not in probabilities. Who value what we have more than what we might get. Who make snap judgments that are often right, and sometimes disastrously wrong, but who can learn to adjust our environment so that the snap judgments point in a better direction.

Kahneman and Tversky did not solve the puzzle of human irrationality. They did something more important. They showed that the puzzle itself was framed wrong. The question isn’t “Why are we so illogical?” The question is “What kind of logic are we using?”[^1]

And the answer, it turns out, is a logic that is exquisitely suited to the world we grew up in—a world of immediate threats and social bonds, where the present mattered more than the distant future. That logic looks strange in the world of spreadsheets and stock markets, but it is not a bug. It is the operating system we have been given. The trick is to understand it, and to build the world around it accordingly.

Which brings us back to that disease problem. The reason people chose so differently in the two frames is not that they were stupid. It is that the first frame—saving two hundred people—appealed to our desire to do good. The second frame—four hundred people will die—activated our fear of loss. Both are valid forms of human reasoning. Behavioral economics doesn’t say we should ignore the second one. It says we should *expect* it, and design our choices accordingly. Humans are not broken. They just need a better frame.[^2]

[^1]: There is a long history of resistance to behavioral economics within mainstream economics. The assumption of rationality was so deeply embedded that any deviation was seen as an anomaly. But as Thaler has noted, the real anomaly was the model itself. In 2017, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for his contributions to behavioral economics—a sign that the field had finally been accepted.

[^2]: Some critics argue that behavioral economics is just a fancy name for common sense. But common sense does not tell you that changing the default from opt-in to opt-out can raise savings rates by 30 percentage points. That is specific, testable, and practical. The difference between common sense and a scientific discovery is data.
