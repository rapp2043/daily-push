SUBJECT LINE: Why Music Majors Vote More Than Political Science Students

Michael P. McDonald, a political scientist at the University of Florida, spends his life counting people who don’t show up. His specialty is voter turnout, and for years, he has tracked a persistent, puzzling gap. College students, for all their protests and passions, have historically voted at lower rates than almost any other demographic. Professors lament it. Pundits pontificate about apathy. The assumption is that the campus quad, for all its ideological energy, is a civic desert. But then, a few years ago, researchers began slicing the data differently. They stopped asking about “college students” as a monolith and started looking at what those students were actually studying. And what they found in the course catalog turned the assumption about youth and apathy on its head.

We like to think we know who cares about democracy. It’s the person with the “Political Science” textbook under their arm, debating constitutional theory. It’s the future lawyer in the pre-law club. These are the people who talk about politics, who follow the news, who we assume will naturally translate that interest into a ballot. They are the obvious citizens. But here’s the problem with that theory: it’s wrong. Or at least, incomplete in a startling way.

The most comprehensive data on this comes from the National Study of Learning, Voting, and Engagement, housed at Tufts University’s Jonathan M. Tisch College of Civic Life. They don’t just ask students if they voted; they anonymize and match student enrollment records with public voting files. This is hard data, not self-reported intention. And when they break down turnout rates by major, the results are not what you’d predict. The highest voting rates don’t belong to the government majors. In fact, they’re often outvoted by their peers in fields we rarely associate with the ballot box.

Think agriculture. Think music. Think something called “interdisciplinary studies.” According to the NSLVE’s multi-year analyses, these are consistently among the top majors for voter turnout. In the 2020 election, while the national student average was around 66%, these fields surged past 70% and even 80% at some institutions. Meanwhile, some traditionally “civic” fields like history or economics often land in the middle of the pack. Political science does fine, but it’s rarely the champion. So, we’re left with a central question: Why does the student in the practice room, rehearsing a concerto, or the one in the field, studying soil science, become a more reliable citizen than the student analyzing electoral maps?

The answer, it turns out, has very little to do with political knowledge and everything to do with *practice*. Claire B. Wofford, a political scientist at the College of Charleston, dug into this paradox in a 2021 paper. She argued that the link isn’t about the *content* of the major, but about its *pedagogy* and *community*. Majors with high voting rates, she found, often share two features. First, they are frequently built around hands-on, applied learning—what academics call “high-impact practices.” Second, they tend to be tight-knit communities where students feel a strong sense of belonging and mutual obligation.

Consider the agriculture student. Their coursework isn’t just in a lecture hall; it’s on a university farm, in a cooperative extension office, at a county fair. They are taught, explicitly and implicitly, that their work exists within a system—a system of regulations, subsidies, land use policies, and environmental stewardship. A change in Farm Bill isn’t an abstract political concept; it’s a shift in the reality of their future livelihood. Voting becomes a direct, practical act of participation in that system. It’s a extension of the applied work they do every day.[^1]

Now, take the music major. On the surface, Chopin has nothing to do with Congress. But look closer. A music department is a collective enterprise. An orchestra or a jazz ensemble only functions through intense cooperation, listening, and a shared responsibility to the group. You show up for rehearsal not just for yourself, but for the cellist next to you, for the conductor, for the audience. This culture of obligation—of showing up because others are counting on you—translates more easily to civic duty than the solitary acquisition of political facts. The act of voting is, in essence, showing up for your community. The music major has been rehearsing that very concept for years.

This is where our political science major might falter. Their education is often brilliantly analytical, focused on systems, theories, and critiques. They become experts in how the machinery is *supposed* to work, and more acutely, in all the ways it is broken. This can, paradoxically, lead to a kind of cynical disconnect. When you see all the gears and rust, the simple act of pulling a lever can feel insignificant, even naïve. The political theorist contemplates the ideal democracy; the agriculture student experiences the tangible one.

Let’s play a quick thought experiment. Imagine two students on a rainy Tuesday, Election Day. One is a senior poli-sci major, bogged down with a thesis on voter suppression tactics. The other is a sophomore in the forestry program, who just spent the weekend with her cohort planting trees in a local park. Which one is more likely to feel that showing up at the precinct, despite the rain, is a meaningful, necessary part of their day? The data suggests it’s the one whose education is rooted in applied service, not the one whose education is mired in systemic critique.

This isn’t to say political knowledge is worthless. It is vital. But it suggests that the *habit* of participation is cultivated in different soil than we thought. It’s nurtured not in the debate about ideals, but in the practice of community. The music department, the agriculture school, the interdisciplinary program that forces students to solve real-world problems—these are incubators for a sense of reciprocal duty. They teach that you are part of a whole, and the whole suffers if you don’t contribute your part.

So, back to Professor McDonald, counting the missing. The story his data now tells is more nuanced, and more hopeful, than the old tale of youthful apathy. It says that the will to participate isn’t dying on campus; it’s just flourishing in unexpected gardens. The lesson for those who want a more engaged citizenry isn’t to force-feed more political theory. It might be to create more experiences that feel like an orchestra or a field study—where the individual success is inextricably tied to the health of the collective, and where showing up is the first, fundamental note you play.

[^1]: This connects to a broader, fascinating thread in social science called the "Halo Effect" of applied learning. Researchers like the late Phil Rosenzweig have written about how direct, tangible tasks create a cognitive bias toward seeing connected systems as manageable and worth engaging with, as opposed to abstract systems which feel distant and uncontrollable. The farm isn't just a plot of land; it's a node in a visible network.
[^2]: There's an important caveat here about institutional context. The NSLVE data shows that voting rates vary wildly *between* colleges, often more than they do between majors within a single college. A "civic desert" campus, as researchers at Tufts call them, can depress turnout across all majors. So the music major at a school that prioritizes and facilitates engagement will still far outvote the music major at a school that does not. The major provides the motivation; the institution must provide the accessible pathway.
