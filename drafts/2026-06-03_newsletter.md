SUBJECT LINE: The Problem with Software Isn’t Code Anymore. It’s Something Far More Boring.

Lisa is a product manager at a mid-sized SaaS company. On a Tuesday morning in March, she sits in the weekly engineering prioritization meeting, watching the same argument unfold for the fourth month running. Her team has just shipped a feature that took six weeks to build. It’s beautiful. The engineers are proud. The code is clean. And when they run the usage data, nobody uses it. Not because it’s buggy, but because it’s the wrong thing. The engineers turn to Lisa: “We built exactly what you asked for.” She stares at the screen. She knows they’re right. But what was the point of building it if the problem was never about how fast they could code?

For decades, the software industry has believed that execution matters most. Get the engineering pipeline moving faster. Remove blockers. Hire more developers. The logic is locked in place like a concrete slab: if we can just write code faster, we’ll win. That assumption is so deeply embedded that entire companies—and entire fortunes—have been built on tools that shave milliseconds off build times. But there’s a problem with that theory.

Research published earlier this year by airfocus and Lucid Research suggests that the real bottleneck in software has quietly migrated. According to their work, the most painful constraint is no longer the gap between *having an idea* and *shipping code*. It’s the gap between *shipping code* and *knowing whether that code is the right code*. Engineering throughput is up. Product alignment is down. And the numbers make you stop.

Here is the puzzle you have to solve. When airfocus and Lucid surveyed product and engineering leaders, they found that something like half of all development effort goes into features that don’t move the needle.[^1] Not because the engineers are slow. Because the organization cannot agree on what needle to move. The bottleneck has shifted from the technical floor to the strategic ceiling.

Think about what that means for a moment. The tools we use to measure developer productivity—lines of code, pull request velocity, deployment frequency—they all measure how fast we can travel in a given direction. But they are completely silent on whether that direction is the right one. It’s like measuring the speed of a car without asking if the car is pointed at the right city. You can go a hundred miles an hour, but if you’re heading east when you should be going west, you end up farther from your destination faster.

Here’s where the Gladwellian flip happens. For a long time, we assumed better engineering meant better products. That was true in an era when software was simple and customer needs were predictable. But software is no longer simple, and customer needs change by the hour. The people who build the code can now build almost anything. The bottleneck is no longer *can we build it?* but *should we build it?* And that second question is, by its nature, a product alignment question—one that requires constant, boring, human judgment.

Take the case of a different company. A startup I’ll call DataFlow (not its real name) spent eighteen months and several million dollars building a data analytics platform. The engineering team was world-class. They delivered on time, under budget, with zero critical bugs. The platform launched. And within three months it was dead. Why? Because the CEO had decided the product strategy six months before launch, and in that six months the market had changed. The team was so efficient that they built the wrong thing faster than anyone else. Speed amplified error.

What the airfocus research suggests is that the root cause of this failure isn’t laziness or incompetence. It’s structural. In a typical software organization, the product team decides *what* to build, the engineering team decides *how* to build it, and the two groups speak different languages. Product talks in terms of user stories and market opportunities. Engineering talks in terms of sprints and technical debt. The translation between those languages is where the alignment breaks down—and AI hasn’t fixed that. If anything, by making engineering faster, AI has widened the gap.

Consider what happens when a product manager asks for a feature. Before AI, an engineer might say, “That will take three weeks—let’s simplify.” That negotiation forced alignment. Now, with AI-assisted coding tools, the engineer might say, “Actually, I can prototype that in two days.” The alignment conversation disappears. The feature gets built. And the product manager still doesn’t know if it’s the right feature. Faster building can mask slower thinking.

So what’s the solution? According to the research, it isn’t more engineering tools. It’s better product practices: clearer prioritization frameworks, tighter feedback loops between builders and users, and an organizational structure that forces the product and engineering teams to share the same definition of “done.” In other words, the bottleneck is now a communication problem disguised as a productivity problem.

I said earlier that the conventional wisdom says execution matters most. That wisdom is not wrong—it’s just outdated. In a world where AI can generate code faster than a human can type, execution becomes table stakes. The differentiator becomes judgment. And judgment cannot be automated. It has to be cultivated, measured, and rewarded. That’s the real insight from the airfocus study: the software industry has traded one bottleneck for another, and most organizations are still optimizing for the old one.

Let’s go back to Lisa, the product manager from the opening. If she could snap her fingers and make her engineering team twice as fast, would that solve her problem? Not really. Her problem isn’t that the code is slow. It’s that she doesn’t know which code to write. The research suggests that the most valuable thing she can do is spend less time asking “How fast can we build this?” and more time asking “How do we know this is the right thing to build?” That shift is deeply uncomfortable for an industry that worships speed. But it’s the only way to stop building things nobody wants.

[^1]: This figure is drawn from the airfocus/Lucid Research survey results as reported in the press release. The specific number might vary by company size and industry, but the pattern is consistent: a substantial fraction of engineering output goes unused or unvalued.
