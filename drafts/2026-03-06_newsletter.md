SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Green Fuel Might Be Making Things Worse

Christian Bruch, the CEO of Siemens Energy, stood in a Berlin conference hall last year and made a confession that seemed to defy his company’s entire business plan. Siemens Energy, a titan in the world of power generation, was betting billions on hydrogen as the clean fuel of the future. Yet Bruch told his audience that the current global push for so-called “green” hydrogen was, in his words, “a bubble.” The problem wasn’t the technology. The problem was everything around it. “We need to be clear,” he said, “that this is not going to work from today to tomorrow.” Here was a man selling the shovels, warning the gold rushers that the mine might collapse.

We’re told a simple story about green hydrogen. Take renewable energy, use it to split water into hydrogen and oxygen, and you have a clean-burning fuel that can power trucks, make steel, and store energy for a cloudy day. It’s the missing piece for the hard-to-decarbonize parts of our economy. The narrative is so compelling that governments from Washington to Brussels have committed hundreds of billions of dollars in subsidies to kickstart the industry. The assumption is straightforward: build the hydrogen electrolyzers, and the clean energy will follow. It’s a solution that matches the scale of our climate problem.

But here’s the problem with that theory. It puts the cart before the horse in a way that could make our emissions problem worse.

A recent study, highlighted by researchers at the think tank Carbon Tracker, lays out the paradox with startling clarity. Green hydrogen is only as clean as the electricity used to produce it. If you build a massive hydrogen plant today and plug it into a grid that still runs on coal and natural gas—which describes most grids on Earth—you are not creating a green fuel. You are creating a monstrously inefficient fossil fuel product. To make one unit of energy from green hydrogen, you need to put in about three to four units of renewable electricity, thanks to conversion losses. If that input electricity is dirty, you end up emitting *more* carbon than if you had just burned the natural gas directly. The very drive to scale up quickly, without first cleaning up the electrical supply chain, could backfire spectacularly. The study’s conclusion is blunt: without a simultaneous, radical overhaul of global electricity grids to run on renewables, the green hydrogen dream becomes an emissions nightmare.

This feels like a new, technical problem born of our modern climate urgency. But we’ve seen this movie before. It’s a story about infrastructure, and how we chronically underestimate it.

Consider the great promise of the 1970s: ethanol. In the wake of the oil crisis, corn-based ethanol was going to make America energy independent. It was a homegrown, renewable fuel. Billions were invested. But the environmental math was quietly brutal. The narrative focused on the clean burn of the ethanol itself, while ignoring the colossal supply chain behind it—the fossil fuels used to farm the corn, fertilize it, harvest it, transport it, and run the distillation plants. For years, critics argued that the total energy input might be greater than the output, making it a net negative. The point wasn’t that the idea was inherently wrong; it was that the infrastructure surrounding it wasn’t ready to make it right. The story was about the fuel. The reality was about the farm, the truck, and the refinery.

The hydrogen puzzle is the ethanol dilemma on steroids and with a ticking clock. The lesson is that you cannot have a clean end-product without a clean beginning. The *supply chain is the product*.

So what would you do? Imagine you’re a governor with a billion dollars in federal hydrogen hub money. You can lure a gleaming new electrolyzer factory to your state, promising thousands of jobs and a place in the clean energy future. The ribbon-cutting photo is irresistible. Or, you can spend that billion upgrading transmission lines, subsidizing local solar microgrids, and modernizing a decrepit substation. It’s invisible, bureaucratic, and politically thankless. Which do you choose? Our entire policy apparatus is designed to choose the factory. The incentive is to produce hydrogen *now*, not to ensure the electricity is green *first*.

This is the hidden trap of technological optimism. We fall in love with the elegant machine—the hydrogen fuel cell, the electrolyzer—and forget the messy, ugly, unglamorous world it must sit inside. The engineers at Siemens Energy can design a perfect electrolyzer, but they cannot, by themselves, decarbonize the power grid of Ohio or Gujarat. That requires a different kind of work: political, regulatory, and financial. It’s about strings of permits, not strings of molecules.

And so we return to Christian Bruch, the CEO warning of a bubble. His confession wasn’t an indictment of hydrogen. It was an indictment of our timeline. He was pointing to the chasm between the investor decks full of green futures and the present-day reality of a carbon-soaked grid. He was seeing the ethanol playbook repeat itself. A truly green hydrogen economy isn’t just about building factories. It’s about rewiring the world, first. The irony is profound: to create the fuel of the future, we must first fix the past’s most stubborn problem—how we make electricity. The shiny new thing depends entirely on fixing the old, boring thing we’ve been neglecting for decades.

The universal lesson is that no solution exists in a vacuum. Every revolution is, at its heart, an infrastructure project.

[^1]: This isn't just theoretical. In 2023, a major green hydrogen project in the western United States was delayed for years because it couldn’t secure a guarantee that the new renewable energy it needed wouldn’t simply be offset by increased fossil fuel use elsewhere on the grid—a problem known as “additionality.” The most difficult hurdles aren't technical; they are accounting problems.
[^2]: The historical parallel goes even deeper. In the early 20th century, the adoption of electric household appliances was severely limited until the Roosevelt administration’s massive rural electrification projects built the underlying grid. The demand for the tool had to wait for the network to be built.
