SUBJECT LINE: Why You Already Do Cultural Studies Without Knowing It

In the spring of 2014, a young British writer named Reni Eddo-Lodge sat down to write a blog post. She was frustrated. For years, she had been trying to explain the particular texture of racial bias in Britain to white friends and colleagues—the quiet assumptions, the structural barriers, the history that everyone seemed to have forgotten. The conversations kept hitting the same wall. The response was often a defensive, “I don’t see race,” or a weary, “Isn’t this all in the past?” That day, she typed out a sentence that would later become the title of a bestselling book: “I’m no longer talking to white people about race.”[^1] It wasn’t a statement of surrender, but one of exhausted clarity. She had encountered a system of thinking—or, more accurately, of *not* thinking—that was so ingrained, so assumed, it had become invisible to those it benefited. Eddo-Lodge wasn’t just hitting a communication barrier; she was bumping up against the silent architecture of culture itself.

We tend to think of “culture” as the visible stuff: the art we consume, the holidays we celebrate, the food we eat. It’s heritage, tradition, a backdrop to our lives. Studying it, then, seems like a soft, almost decorative pursuit—the analysis of novels, films, and pop songs. It’s what you do in university seminars that seem disconnected from the urgent pressures of the real world. But here’s the problem with that assumption: it mistakes the decoration for the building. What if culture isn’t just the paintings on the wall, but the wall itself? What if the most powerful forces in our lives are the silent agreements we never made, the stories we never realized we were telling, the hierarchies we accept as just “the way things are”?

To understand what Eddo-Lodge was really confronting, we have to rewind to a specific, damp place and time: a series of cramped, makeshift offices at the University of Birmingham in England, in the late 1950s and 60s. This was the birthplace of something called the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies. Its founder, a brilliant Jamaican-born thinker named Stuart Hall, arrived in Britain as a Rhodes scholar and found a society profoundly confused about itself. The old imperial power was fading, a new youth culture was exploding, and waves of immigration from the Caribbean and South Asia were challenging a monolithic, and mythical, idea of “Britishness.” Hall looked at this tumult and saw something others missed. He wasn’t interested in culture as a canon of great works. He was interested in culture as a **battleground**—the place where meanings are made, contested, and where power is reinforced or resisted.

Think of the last advertisement you saw, or the plot of a popular television show. Hall would ask: What is this *really* saying? Not just about shampoo or relationships, but about gender, success, race, and class? Who is portrayed as heroic, and who is sidelined? What version of the “good life” is being sold, and who is excluded from it? This practice—pulling apart the everyday “texts” of our lives to see the hidden wiring—was the core of what Hall and his colleagues developed. They studied everything from motorcycle subcultures to television news, not to celebrate them or condemn them, but to **decode** them. They argued that the media doesn’t just reflect reality; it actively produces our understanding of it. The news isn’t a window on the world; it’s a careful construction, a narrative that frames who matters, what counts as a problem, and what solutions are thinkable.[^2]

This is where the idea of “taken-for-granted” assumptions gets dismantled. Raymond Williams, another key figure in this tradition, called these assumptions “structures of feeling”—the lived, almost intangible experience of a particular time and place that shapes how we perceive everything before we even begin to think critically. The feeling that certain careers are “for” certain people. The unease when a familiar narrative is challenged. The defensive “I don’t see race” is a perfect example: it assumes that neutrality is possible and that noticing race is the problem, rather than examining the systems built upon racial categories. Cultural studies insisted that these feelings weren’t natural or accidental; they were cultivated. They were the product of stories, images, laws, and economic arrangements that, over time, came to feel like common sense.

So let’s play a game. Take a piece of culture so ubiquitous it’s almost background noise: a mainstream evening news broadcast. Watch it not for *what* is said, but for *how* it’s said. Who gets to speak as an authority? Is it a politician, a professor, a CEO, or a community organizer? When the camera shows a “crowd,” who is in it? What neighborhoods are depicted as dangerous, and which are depicted as idyllic? What problems are presented as technical issues to be solved by experts, and what problems are presented as moral failings of individuals? You are now doing cultural studies. You are no longer just receiving information; you are investigating the machinery of its production.

This brings us back to Reni Eddo-Lodge and her blog post. She wasn’t simply failing to communicate. She was trying to point out the hidden wiring in the British cultural narrative—the stories of empire that had been whitewashed, the economic structures that perpetuated inequality, the daily micro-aggressions that reinforced a racial hierarchy. Her white interlocutors were often operating within a “structure of feeling” that rendered those things invisible. Their common sense said, “We are a post-racial society,” because the culture they consumed reinforced that message by omission and framing. The explosive response to her work—the bestselling book, the podcast, the intense debates—proved her central point: talking about race wasn’t just about personal prejudice; it was about forcing a reckoning with a national story. It was an act of cultural decoding on a massive scale.

The legacy of those cramped offices in Birmingham is not an academic niche. It’s a fundamental tool for navigating the modern world. Whenever you wonder why a certain idea feels unshakeable, why an injustice seems so stubborn, or why a change in language feels so threatening, you are brushing up against culture as a battleground. The activists dissecting media bias, the marketers trying to tap into a generation’s “vibe,” the employees questioning their company’s unspoken norms—they are all, in their way, practicing cultural studies. It is the study of the water we swim in, undertaken at the moment we realize we are swimming. It is the practice of making the invisible visible, not to paralyze us, but to show us that what was built by story can be rebuilt by a better one. Eddo-Lodge stopped talking to white people about race not because the conversation was over, but because she had understood that the real conversation had to start much earlier. It had to start with the silent stories we all already knew by heart, but had never learned to read.

[^1]: That blog post, published in February 2014, would become the foundation for her 2017 book *Why I’m No Longer Talking to White People About Race*, which spent weeks atop the UK bestseller lists and fundamentally shifted the national conversation on race and history.
[^2]: Hall’s most famous model for this is called “encoding/decoding.” He argued that a media producer “encodes” a message with a preferred meaning, rooted in their own assumptions and the broader social context. The audience, however, can “decode” it in three ways: they can accept the preferred meaning, negotiate a mixed interpretation, or offer a full “oppositional” reading that rejects the underlying framework. The meaning, therefore, is never fixed at the point of production; it is completed in the struggle of interpretation.
