SUBJECT LINE: The Nobel Prize That Rewrote the Rules of the Street

When the phone call came, Robert Sampson was likely thinking about trees. Or parks. Or the way a cracked sidewalk in front of a boarded-up building tells a story that a crime statistic never could. The call was from Stockholm, informing him he had won the 2024 Stockholm Prize in Criminology, an award so prestigious it’s often called the field’s equivalent of a Nobel. The official citation praised his work for “revolutionizing our understanding of the relationship between crime and place.” But that dry, academic phrase doesn’t capture the quiet earthquake Sampson’s research represents. For decades, we’ve been telling a particular story about why crime happens where it does. Sampson, a professor at UC Irvine, proved that story was almost completely backwards.

Our conventional story about neighborhood crime is, fundamentally, a story about bad people. High-crime areas, the logic goes, are plagued by a concentration of criminal individuals. The solution, then, is to identify and remove those individuals. This thinking propelled the era of “broken windows” policing and mass incarceration. It’s an intuitive, person-centric model: find the rotten apples, and you save the barrel. But here’s the problem with that theory. It can’t explain a simple, persistent finding: when you ask people to *draw* crime on a map of their city, the maps from Boston, Chicago, and Los Angeles look almost identical, even though the people living in those high-crime zones are completely different. The pattern is about place, not population.

To understand Sampson’s revolution, we have to go back to where the old story began. In the 1940s, a pair of sociologists at the University of Chicago, Clifford Shaw and Henry McKay, made a fascinating observation. They mapped juvenile delinquency in Chicago and found the highest rates were always in the city’s core, regardless of which immigrant group—Irish, Italian, Polish—currently lived there. As one group moved out to better neighborhoods, their delinquency rates dropped. The new group moving in would see their rates spike. Shaw and McKay called this “social disorganization.” The problem, they argued, was the neighborhood itself—its poverty, its transience, its lack of social glue. This was a radical, place-based idea. But over time, it got simplified and moralized. The “disorganization” became a character flaw of the residents, not a condition of their environment. The focus shifted from crumbling infrastructure to corrupt morals.

Sampson’s genius was to return to that original Chicago School insight with a modern toolbox and a simple, devastating question: what if a neighborhood isn’t just a backdrop for crime, but an active *producer* of social outcomes? What if place itself has a character? For over two decades, he led the Project on Human Development in Chicago Neighborhoods, an unprecedented study that combined systematic social observation (teams filming city blocks), surveys, and police data. He wasn’t just counting crimes; he was measuring a neighborhood’s personality.

And what he found overturned everything. Crime wasn’t best predicted by poverty alone, or even by the concentration of “criminal” individuals. The strongest predictor was something he called “collective efficacy.” That’s a technical term for a simple idea: the willingness of neighbors to trust each other and to intervene for the common good. Would you stop kids from spray-painting a wall? Would you ask a noisy group to quiet down? Would you feel comfortable asking a neighbor to keep an eye on your package?[^1] Sampson and his team could measure this social fabric. And they found that two neighborhoods with identical poverty rates could have wildly different crime rates based on this single, crucial factor. The invisible script of a street—written by trust, mutual expectation, and a sense of shared ownership—was more powerful than any police patrol.

This is where the story flips. We thought neighborhoods were dangerous because bad people lived there. Sampson showed that sometimes, “bad” people behave differently because they live in a neighborhood that is, socially, disintegrating. The causal arrow points the other way. A place with low collective efficacy isn’t just vulnerable to crime; it actively generates the conditions for it. It fails to supervise teens. It isolates the elderly. It allows small signs of disorder—that broken window—to bloom because no one feels empowered or connected enough to fix it. The environment teaches its lessons relentlessly.

So what creates collective efficacy? It’s not magic. Sampson’s work points to tangible, place-based factors. Stability matters—people who expect to stay invest more. Homeownership correlates strongly. But so do public spaces: the presence of vibrant parks, community centers, and even well-kept sidewalks where people naturally gather. It’s about designing a stage where positive social interaction isn’t just possible, but inevitable. This is a far cry from the policy lever of simply adding more police. It’s about growing the social soil itself.
