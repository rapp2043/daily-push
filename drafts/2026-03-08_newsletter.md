SUBJECT LINE: The Whisper in the Machine

Q. Vera Liao, a researcher studying how we understand artificial intelligence, began with a simple question. She and her team asked a large language model—a chatbot like ChatGPT—to explain its reasoning. They gave it a scenario: a person is choosing what to eat and picks sushi. The AI was asked to explain why. The answer was telling. The model generated an explanation rooted in personal health and individual choice: sushi is nutritious, it’s a lean protein, it offers a mindful eating experience. It was a perfectly reasonable, utterly Western explanation. But Liao, who studies the cultural dimensions of technology, saw something else. She saw a ghost in the machine. The explanation wasn’t just an answer; it was a worldview, packaged as logic. And it made her wonder: when an AI explains itself, whose voice are we really hearing?

We like to think of AI explanations as clean, neutral things. They are the “show your work” of the machine mind, a transparent ledger of logic meant to build trust. In a world worried about algorithmic black boxes, this explainability is touted as the solution—a way to ensure fairness and accountability. It’s a comforting narrative. The machine shows us its math, and we, rational actors, can judge its conclusions. But that’s not quite right. What Liao and her colleagues found in their research, published earlier this year, is that these explanations are not culturally blank slates. They come pre-loaded with a perspective. When they analyzed the explanations generated by several popular large language models, a pattern emerged: a systematic preference for reasoning that aligns with values common in Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic societies—what social scientists sometimes call WEIRD cultures. The models defaulted to explanations that emphasized personal autonomy, individual choice, and analytical cause-and-effect. They whispered in a distinctly Western accent.

But then, consider a different way of explaining the world. Imagine a grandmother in Tokyo, presented with the same sushi choice. Her explanation might weave together a different tapestry of reasons. It might consider the seasonality of the fish, a concept known as *shun*. It might factor in the chef’s recommendation, a deference to expertise and social harmony. It might reference family tradition, or a sense of shared experience. The explanation is no less valid, but its architecture is different. It is less an isolated bullet point of individual benefit and more a node in a web of social and contextual connections. This is the kind of reasoning that the AI, in its current form, consistently undervalues or ignores in its explanatory outputs. It doesn’t just *make* a choice with potential bias; it *explains* that choice using a culturally narrow dictionary of motives.

So why does this happen? The answer lies not in the present, but in a much older story about how we organize knowledge. To understand the AI’s Western whisper, we have to go back to the whisperers: the people who built the frameworks that shape modern thought. For centuries, Western missionaries and colonial administrators traveled the world with a dual mandate: to convert and to catalog. They encountered languages and cultures that organized reality in profoundly different ways. A famous example lies in the work of early missionary linguists. When they set about translating the Bible or creating grammars for indigenous languages, they didn’t simply map words. They imposed structures. They forced verb tenses that implied a linear, progressive timeline onto languages that conceived of time as cyclical or event-based. They created dictionaries that categorized the world into nouns and verbs, objects and actions, often missing concepts that existed in a space between those categories. They were, in essence, creating the first large-scale training data sets for cross-cultural understanding, and they baked their own worldview directly into the codex.

This historical process didn’t just change languages; it changed ways of thinking. The analytical, categorical, and individual-centric mode of reasoning became the sanctioned mode of reasoning, the one written down, taught in schools, and eventually encoded into the logic of early computing. Fast forward to the 21st century, and we are doing something startlingly similar. We feed our most powerful AI models a diet of text that is overwhelmingly produced in and shaped by WEIRD cultures. The internet, for all its global reach, is linguistically and culturally skewed. English dominates, and with it comes a substrate of implicit values. The model learns that a “good” explanation—one that is coherent, persuasive, and logically structured—often looks like the explanations found in an American corporate report, a Western philosophy paper, or a European self-help book. It learns to prioritize the individual actor over the collective context. It learns to value explicit, linear causality over holistic, situational reasoning. The AI isn’t being malicious; it’s being a mirror. It has ingested a historical bias and is now reproducing it, not in its decisions alone, but in its very justifications for those decisions.

Here’s a question for you: if you asked an AI to explain why a family in a collectivist culture might choose a certain career path for their child, what would it say? Would it talk about filial piety, family honor, and social obligation with the same fluency as it talks about personal passion, individual talent, and market demand? The research suggests it would struggle. The bias isn’t in the outcome—the AI might correctly “predict” the choice—but in the *story it tells* about that outcome. The explanation becomes a vehicle for cultural transmission, and right now, it’s running a one-way route.

This is the deeper puzzle Liao’s work points to. The concern isn't merely that an AI might be biased, but that its *explanations for its own behavior* are culturally myopic. This creates a dangerous illusion of understanding. A doctor in Nairobi or a farmer in Kerala might receive an AI-generated explanation for a loan denial or a medical diagnosis that feels alien, that fails to resonate with their own framework of cause and effect, responsibility, and society. They are told the machine is being transparent, but the logic it speaks is a foreign language. Trust isn’t built; it’s eroded. The very tool meant to democratize understanding may end up enforcing a single, standardized way of thinking about why things happen.

And so we return to Vera Liao and her question about sushi. That simple test was a probe into the soul of our new machines. It revealed that the “why” is as culturally constructed as the “what.” The AI’s explanation was a tiny, perfect artifact of a centuries-old project to make the world’s knowledge conform to a particular shape. The ghost in the machine isn't a phantom; it's an echo. It’s the echo of missionary grammars, Enlightenment categories, and the deep, often invisible, architecture of Western thought. We built these systems to explain themselves to us. But first, we have to listen very carefully to the accent.

[^1]: The choice of sushi as a test case is itself interesting. It’s a food that has been globally adopted but retains strong cultural specificities. The AI’s “Westernized” explanation of it strips away that context, treating it as just another commodity in a global pantry of individual choice—a perfect miniature of the broader phenomenon.
[^2]: It’s critical to note that the study did not find the AI’s underlying decisions to be more or less accurate across cultures; the bias was isolated to the *explanatory style*. This separates the logic of the outcome from the narrative of the justification, a distinction we often blur in human affairs as well.
