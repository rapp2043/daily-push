SUBJECT LINE: Why Every Solution Breaks the World in a New Way

In the spring of 1928, a chemist named Thomas Midgley Jr. stepped before the American Chemical Society in a crowded hotel ballroom. He held a simple jar of clear liquid. To demonstrate its extraordinary safety, he took a deep breath and poured it over his hands. Then he inhaled its vapors deeply, like a man enjoying sea air. The substance was dichlorodifluoromethane, a compound he had helped invent for the Frigidaire corporation. The world would come to know it as Freon, the miracle coolant that would make household refrigerators—and later, air conditioning—safe and commonplace. Midgley was hailed as a hero, a man who had banished the dangerous, explosive gases used in early cooling systems. He received awards; his invention spread across the globe. It was a perfect story of a problem identified and elegantly solved by human ingenuity.[^1]

We tell ourselves a story about technology. We identify a problem—food spoilage, distance, ignorance, discomfort. Clever people in labs or garages devise a tool to solve it. The tool spreads, the problem recedes, and society marches forward, better than before. It’s a narrative of control, of intention. We build things *for* a purpose, and we expect them to serve that purpose. But what if that story is mostly wrong? What if, for every intended consequence of our tools, there are cascading waves of unintended ones that we never see coming? And what if those unintended consequences aren’t just minor side-effects, but the main event?

This is the curious argument made by writer and historian Edward Tenner. He suggests that the history of innovation is less a history of plans achieved and more a history of surprises delivered. We focus on the bright line of intention—the fridge that keeps food cold—and miss the sprawling, shadowy landscape of effects that the fridge creates: new chemical industries, changes in domestic labor, shifts in diet, and, in Freon’s case, a gaping hole in the planetary ozone layer. The unintended consequence isn’t a footnote; it is, in Tenner’s view, often the more profound chapter. It turns out that our world is built less by our designs than by the reverberations of those designs echoing through complex systems we barely understand.

We can think of these surprises in three broad categories. The first is the *environmental rebound*. This is where solving a problem in one domain inadvertently creates a worse problem in another, often by unlocking new levels of consumption. Take the very invention Midgley championed. By making refrigeration safe and cheap, Freon didn’t just preserve food; it helped enable the modern supermarket, the global food supply chain, and the year-round expectation of fresh produce. It changed how we lived and what we ate. But the chemical itself, so stable and non-toxic in the human realm, was catastrophically stable in the upper atmosphere. Each molecule of Freon could destroy tens of thousands of ozone molecules, the planet’s protective layer against ultraviolet radiation. The solution to a kitchen problem became a existential threat to the biosphere—a threat only mitigated decades later by a global treaty to ban the very substance that was once showered with awards. The efficiency created a new, larger scale of operation, which in turn created a new, larger scale of vulnerability.

The second category is the *social reconfiguration*. Here, a tool invented for one purpose quietly rewires human relationships, power structures, and social norms. Consider the social media “Like” button. Its intended consequence was simple and benign: to give users a quick, low-friction way to express positive feedback, to make the platform feel more friendly and engaging. A tiny digital pat on the back. But what behavior did that little heart or thumbs-up actually incentivize? It created a public, quantifiable currency of approval. It turned amorphous social interaction into a game of scorekeeping. It began to dictate what content was created, pushing creators toward outrage, conformity, or performative emotion—whatever triggered the most measurable validation. The unintended consequence was not just occasional anxiety; it was the subtle reshaping of public discourse, teen psychology, and political activism into forms optimized for engagement metrics, not truth or civic health. The tool designed to connect us became an architecture for ranking us, often making genuine connection harder.

The final category is perhaps the most stubborn: *behavioral resistance*. This is when a perfect technological solution is derailed not by chemistry or algorithms, but by the inconvenient, irrational human heart. A classic example sits in your closet or spins on airport carousels everywhere: the wheeled suitcase. The technology of the wheel is approximately 6,000 years old. The modern suitcase with wheels was patented in 1972 by Bernard Sadow. Yet it was routinely rejected by luggage companies and ignored by travelers for nearly a decade. Why? The intended consequence was obvious: effortless transport. The unintended consequence was social and psychological. It was seen as emasculating—real travelers, especially men, carried their burdens with strength. It was gauche. It marked you as weak or infirm. The solution was technically flawless but culturally anathema. It took an observant Northwest Airlines pilot named Robert Plath, who in 1987 rigged a suitcase with wheels *and a telescoping handle* to create the modern “Rollaboard,” to crack the code. By designing it for flight crews—figures of authority and competence—it gained a new, acceptable identity. The tool didn’t change; the story we told ourselves about the person using it did.

Here’s a thought experiment. Imagine you are Thomas Midgley on that stage in 1928. You have in your hands a compound that will save countless lives from food poisoning and heat stroke. You know its chemistry is inert and safe in every test you can run. How far into the future does your responsibility extend? To the supply chain it enables? To the changes in urban design as people move to hotter climates, sustained by air conditioning? To the chemical reactions in a layer of the atmosphere 15 miles up that no one has even thought to study yet? The answer, of course, is that you can’t. No one can. The chain of unintended consequences is infinite.

This is the humbling core of Tenner’s argument. Our obsession with intention—the neat story of problem and solution—is a kind of vanity. It makes us feel like pilots of the spaceship Earth, when in reality we are more like gardeners, planting seeds in an ecosystem so complex that we can only guess at what will truly sprout and thrive and choke. The wheeled suitcase wasn’t defeated by a technical flaw, but by pride. Social media wasn’t undone by a coding error, but by our ancient craving for status. Freon wasn’t felled by a better coolant, but by its own perfection in a realm its creators never considered.

Which brings us back to Thomas Midgley. His story is almost a parable of unintended consequences. The man who made refrigerators safe also gave the world leaded gasoline, another “solution” (to engine knock) that poisoned generations with neurotoxins. And in a final, darkly ironic twist, later in life he was crippled by polio. He devised an elaborate system of pulleys and ropes to help himself get in and out of bed. In 1944, he became entangled in that very apparatus and died of strangulation. He was, in the end, ultimately and tragically caught in the web of his own designs.

We cannot stop solving problems. But perhaps we can change the story we tell. We are not issuing controlled fixes. We are releasing agents of change into a living, reacting world. The question shifts from “What will this do?” to a more wary, more curious, and more humble: “And then what will happen?”

[^1]: The tragic irony of Midgley’s life is almost novelistic. Celebrated for two inventions that each caused global harm, his personal end reads like a Greek tragedy, where the hero is undone by the mechanics of his own cleverness.
[^2]: The resistance to wheeled luggage is a stunning case of a solution being obvious for millennia, yet socially invisible. It wasn’t that no one thought of it; patents for wheeled trunks exist from the late 19th century. It was that the idea violated a silent code of masculine self-reliance so powerfully that the market refused to see it until it was repackaged—literally—with a new identity.
