SUBJECT LINE: Why Your Best Decisions Are Made On Autopilot

In the spring of 1995, Jerry Richardson, the founder of the NFL’s newest franchise, the Carolina Panthers, faced a critical choice. His team hadn’t even played a game yet, but the league was offering him a rare opportunity: he could draft a proven veteran player from another team to instantly build a competitive roster. The conventional wisdom, the rational analysis, was clear. He should take the sure thing. The proven veteran. The safe bet. But Richardson, a former Colts player who had made his fortune building the Hardee’s and Denny’s restaurant chains, said no. He passed on the immediate talent. Instead, he insisted on doing what he had always done in the restaurant business: he would draft raw, young players and build from the ground up. He chose the longer, harder, seemingly riskier path. It was a decision that baffled the league’s experts. But to Richardson, it wasn’t a decision at all. It was a reflex. A habit of mind forged over decades of flipping burgers and signing franchise agreements.[^1]

We like to think that our most important decisions—where to invest, whom to hire, when to expand—are products of deep, conscious deliberation. We gather data, we weigh pros and cons, we project future outcomes. We imagine ourselves as careful strategists, chess players thinking three moves ahead. It’s a comforting story of rational control. But there’s a problem with that theory. A large body of research, including a significant new study, suggests that this image is largely an illusion. The real engine of our choices, especially the repeated ones that shape our lives and businesses, isn’t conscious strategy. It’s something much more fundamental and, frankly, more mechanical: habit-like repetition.

The new research, a large-scale analysis of decision-making patterns, arrives at a startling conclusion. It finds that the influence of habitual, repetitive patterns on our choices is far stronger than most models of human behavior have allowed for. We are not, it turns out, calculating machines who occasionally fall into routines. We are creatures of routine who occasionally pause to calculate. The study suggests that past repetitions of a behavior create a kind of cognitive pathway so deep and well-worn that future “decisions” simply flow down the channel already carved. The brain, in an effort to conserve its precious energy for novel threats, defaults to the familiar script. What looks from the outside like a considered choice is often just an echo.

But to understand why this finding is so profound, we have to go back. The story of how we came to misunderstand habit begins not in a modern lab, but in the lecture halls of 19th-century Harvard. William James, the father of American psychology, was fascinated by habit. In 1890, he published *The Principles of Psychology*, and in it, he described habit as the “fly-wheel of society,” the enormous, invisible force that keeps the social world running smoothly. He saw it as a positive, liberating force. By automating the trivial—walking, dressing, tying our shoes—habit freed our conscious mind to focus on higher pursuits. For James, the goal of education was to “make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy,” to “form good habits.” He viewed our patterns not as a cognitive failure, but as the very foundation of character.

Then, a few decades later, the story took a turn. Psychologists like Edward Thorndike, working with cats in puzzle boxes, formulated the “law of effect.” Behavior followed by a satisfying consequence, they found, became more likely to be repeated. This was the seed of behaviorism, a school of thought that would come to dominate psychology for much of the 20th century. Behaviorists like B.F. Skinner took this idea and ran with it, focusing almost exclusively on external rewards and punishments. The internal, cognitive experience of a “habit” was irrelevant; all that mattered was the observable stimulus and the observable response. Habit was reduced to a simple, mindless loop. It was something rats did in cages to get pellets. This view seeped into our culture. Habit became a synonym for unthinking routine, a petty vice to be overcome by willpower. The glorious, society-anchoring fly-wheel described by James was recast as a grinding, mundane gear.

This is where the modern puzzle emerges. If the behaviorists were right and habits are just dumb, mechanical responses to rewards, then they should be easily overridden by a shot of conscious motivation or a new piece of information. But anyone who has ever tried to quit smoking, or stick to a diet, or change their morning routine knows that is not true. The habit persists long after the reward is gone and even in the face of compelling reasons to stop. The new research helps explain this stubbornness. It suggests that repetition itself—the sheer number of times we have performed an action in a particular context—builds an independent power. The brain isn’t just seeking a past reward; it is following a well-established protocol. The more you run the protocol, the more it becomes the default setting, separate from any original goal or pleasure.

Let’s play a game. Think about your last trip to the grocery store. You needed milk, bread, and eggs. How did you move through the store? If you’re like most people, you followed a path you’ve followed a hundred times before. You entered, turned right, grabbed a cart, and proceeded along the outer perimeter. You likely ended up with not just the milk, bread, and eggs, but also a few other items you hadn’t planned to buy, plucked from shelves that live along your habitual route. Now, imagine I offered you a hundred dollars if you could exit the store having *only* purchased your three intended items, using the most efficient path possible. Could you do it? You’d have to fight every muscle memory, ignore every familiar sight, and break a script written by countless repetitions. The hundred dollars is the conscious motivation. The labyrinth of habit is the far more powerful force.

This brings us back to Jerry Richardson and the Carolina Panthers. Analysts saw a football decision. They saw a chessboard. But Richardson was in a grocery store he had built himself. His “store”—the business of building something from nothing—had a specific layout. The protocol was clear: acquire raw ingredients (young, hungry players), apply a system (his coaching staff), and scale patiently. The NFL was offering him a pre-made cake in the bakery section. But his cognitive pathway, carved deep by decades of franchising restaurants, led him relentlessly back to the ingredients aisle. He wasn’t choosing a strategy. He was following a groove. And here’s the remarkable thing: it worked. The Panthers, built from Richardson’s habitual blueprint, became the most successful expansion team in NFL history at the time, reaching the NFC Championship in just their second season.

The implication of all this is both unsettling and liberating. It suggests that we are less the authors of our lives than we believe. Our careers, our relationships, our health—they are less a series of distinct, willful choices and more a cumulative product of the tiny, repeated scripts we execute every day, often without a second thought. But if that’s true, then the road to change isn’t necessarily through grand resolutions or sheer willpower. It’s through the painstaking, deliberate redesign of those tiny scripts. We cannot simply decide to be different. We must practice, through repetition, the mechanics of being different, until the new path becomes the default one. The fly-wheel, as William James knew, works both ways. It takes tremendous force to get it spinning in a new direction. But once it is, its own momentum carries it forward, and what once required exhausting thought becomes, at last, a habit.

[^1]: The Panthers’ early success was so dramatic it permanently altered the NFL’s approach to expansion, giving future new teams far less assistance in the draft. Richardson’s habitual model—dismissed as naive—became the league’s new benchmark for how to build a team from zero.

[^2]: The behaviorist focus on external rewards had a colossal impact, shaping everything from education to advertising. It made the science of influence measurable. But in sidelining the internal, cognitive architecture of habit, it may have missed the deeper reason why routines are so tenacious: they become part of the brain’s map of the world itself.
