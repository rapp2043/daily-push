SUBJECT LINE: The Game That Proves Everything We Assume About Sports Is Wrong

We need to start with a free throw. The clock is stopped. The arena is loud, a wall of sound pressing down from the bleachers. Laurie Williams, a guard for the University of Alabama women’s wheelchair basketball team, catches the inbound pass. She doesn’t dribble up the court. She doesn’t need to. With two strong, practiced pushes on the wheels of her chair, she’s at the top of the key. A defender closes in, but Williams is already in motion—a quick spin of her chair, a pivot on one wheel, creating just enough space. She rises, not with her legs, but with the explosive power of her torso and arms, releasing a shot that arcs perfectly through the fluorescent air. Swish.[^1]

We watch sports through a lens of ingrained assumptions. We believe athletic competition is, at its core, a test of innate physical gifts. We see the height of a basketball center, the wingspan of a swimmer, the fast-twitch muscles of a sprinter, and we attribute their success primarily to the genetic lottery. And nowhere is this assumption more entrenched than in the endless, fractious debate about sex differences in sports. The conventional wisdom is straightforward and seemingly unassailable: biological sex creates a permanent, structural advantage for men, rooted in bigger hearts, denser bones, and higher testosterone, making head-to-head competition inherently unfair. It’s a story we tell ourselves that feels as solid as the ground beneath our feet. But what if the ground itself is part of the story? What if we’ve been so focused on the athlete that we’ve completely overlooked the stage?

This is the puzzle that wheelchair basketball places before us. It is a fast, brutal, and breathtakingly skilled sport. Players crash into each other, their titanium chairs clanging like medieval armor. They execute no-look passes and spin moves that would make an NBA point guard blink. And here is the first curious detail: at the elite international level, the sport is fully integrated. Men and women compete on the same teams, for the same spots, in the same leagues. This fact alone should stop us. According to our standard assumptions about physiology, this shouldn’t work. The men, with their greater upper-body strength and mass, should dominate completely, rendering the women peripheral. But they don’t. Watch a game, and you’ll see women not just participating, but often running the offense, taking the clutch shots, and anchoring the defense. The University of Alabama team that Laurie Williams played for was a national powerhouse, and its success was built on a co-ed roster where talent, not sex, was the primary currency. How can this be?

The answer begins not with biology, but with engineering. In wheelchair basketball, the athlete’s body is only part of the system. The other, critical part is the chair. Every aspect of performance is mediated through this machine. Speed isn’t about leg drive; it’s about push-rim mechanics and the efficiency of a gear ratio. Agility isn’t about footwork; it’s about the chair’s axle placement and the player’s ability to shift its center of gravity. The playing field, so to speak, is recalibrated. Raw upper-body strength is still an asset, but it is no longer the sole, overwhelming determinant it is in running basketball. A player with exceptional hand speed, tactical vision, and a deadly outside shot—skills that are far more evenly distributed across sexes—can be more valuable than a pure powerhouse. The game selects for a different constellation of talents.

This idea—that the tools of the sport shape the hierarchy of the athletes—isn’t just philosophical. It’s observable. Consider the three-point shot in professional basketball. For decades, the NBA’s longer three-point line and greater defensive pressure created a style where pure strength and elevation were paramount. The WNBA, with a slightly closer line and (historically) a different defensive landscape, became a laboratory for pure shooting form and off-ball movement. The gap in three-point percentage between the leagues has often been negligible, and sometimes has favored the WNBA. When the NBA finally began to prioritize the three-pointer en masse, they didn’t just find shooters; they found that the *form* of the best shooters often looked more like the fluid, efficient motion honed in the women’s game than the explosive jump shots of the past. The equipment—the distance of the line, the style of play—changed the valued skill set.

We can go further back. Think about the 1948 Olympics and a Dutch sprinter named Fanny Blankers-Koen. A mother of two, she was dubbed “the flying housewife” by a incredulous press. She won four gold medals in track and field, dominating the sprint events. Her secret weapon wasn’t a magical physiology; it was a revolutionary training technique. Her coach, and later husband, Jan Blankers, had her train by running in the soft, heavy sand of Dutch beaches. This resistance training built explosive power that her competitors, who only ran on firm tracks, lacked. Her advantage was technological and methodological, hidden in the sand. The playing field, again, was not level—but it was tilted by innovation, not just by birth.

So what does wheelchair basketball teach us? It proposes a radical and unsettling idea: that many of the performance gaps we attribute to immutable biology are actually amplified, or even created, by the specific physical and mechanical demands we choose to prioritize in a given sport. We built basketball around vertical leap and sustained running, attributes where the average biological difference between sexes is significant. Wheelchair basketball, by accident of its design, built a game around a different set of attributes—core stability, rotational power, tactical intelligence, shooting touch—where the distribution of talent overlaps far more significantly between men and women. The sport didn’t erase biological differences; it simply made them less relevant to the outcome.

This reframes the entire conversation. It moves us from a deterministic argument about fixed bodies to a more interesting question about malleable systems. If changing the equipment or the rules can dramatically alter which physical traits matter most, then the “fairness” of any sport is not a natural law, but a design choice. We have chosen, in most sports, designs that maximize a certain kind of male advantage. But that is our choice, not nature’s decree.

This isn’t just about gender. It’s about how we see all athletic advantage. We fetishize the innate gift, the god-given talent. We love the story of the born superstar. But the deeper, more intriguing story is about the interaction between the human and the context. It’s about how a rule change, a new piece of equipment, or a different training method can suddenly make a previously undervalued set of skills—or a previously overlooked group of people—the key to victory.

Let’s return to that free throw. Laurie Williams isn’t just taking a shot. She is operating a system—her body, her chair, the geometry of the court. Her success in that moment isn’t dictated by a simple biological ledger of male versus female strength. It is determined by a lifetime of mastering a complex, human-made interface. The swoosh of the net is the sound of an assumption being overturned. We look at sports and see a test of nature. But maybe, all along, we’ve been watching something else: a ongoing experiment in design. And the most important variable in that experiment isn’t who is playing the game, but how the game itself is built.

[^1]: The precision of the sport is staggering. The “push” in wheelchair basketball is a highly technical motion. The best players don’t just grab the wheels; they strike them with an open palm, applying force in a perfect, tangential stroke to maximize efficiency and minimize fatigue over a game. It is a skill as refined as a golfer’s swing or a pitcher’s windup.
[^2]: The classification system in wheelchair basketball adds another fascinating layer. Players are given a point value from 1.0 to 4.5 based on their functional ability, with lower-point players having less trunk control. The total point value of all five players on the court cannot exceed 14. This isn’t a medical assessment; it’s a functional one, designed to balance teams and ensure that a variety of physical capacities can find a crucial role. A savvy, low-point guard who can’t lean forward to rebound might be the team’s best ball-handler and shooter. The system, again, forces a reevaluation of what “advantage” means.
