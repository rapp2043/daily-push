SUBJECT LINE: Why Racing Toward the Future Makes You Vulnerable Today

In the spring of 2017, Mark Hilbert, the chief information security officer at a major European bank, faced a problem that felt both urgent and abstract. News headlines were buzzing about quantum computing. Not the science-fiction kind, but real, incremental advances in laboratories from Google to Beijing. His board was asking questions. His peers were forming committees. The message was clear: the cryptographic locks keeping the world’s financial data safe—the complex math that secures everything from a wire transfer to a supplier’s invoice—could be shattered overnight by a sufficiently powerful quantum machine. Hilbert, a careful man with a background in physics, authorized a multi-million-dollar project. His mission was to begin “future-proofing” the bank’s digital infrastructure against this coming storm. He was going to get ahead of the threat.

The instinct is universal. When a disruptive technology appears on the horizon, the early adopters win. We see it in every business school case study: the companies that embraced the internet, that pivoted to mobile, that integrated AI. Lagging behind is a recipe for obsolescence. So, the logic follows, you must sprint toward the next big thing. You must future-proof. This is the reigning dogma in boardrooms and tech conferences today, especially when the topic is quantum computing. The narrative is one of urgent, defensive investment. A recent survey by cybersecurity firm DigiCert found that 61% of global organizations believe they need to be “quantum-ready” by 2030. The race is on.

But here’s the catch. It turns out that in the specific, messy realm of getting ready for a technology that doesn’t fully exist yet, the early sprint might not be the wisest path. New research from the software firm apexanalytix, which helps large companies manage their global suppliers, highlights a peculiar paradox. Their study, which analyzed the preparedness of over 500 major enterprises, suggests that a headlong rush to adopt so-called “post-quantum” cryptographic solutions today could actually *introduce* new and severe vulnerabilities. The very act of future-proofing, if done prematurely or poorly, can make you more exposed, not less. It’s a violation of the classic law of unintended consequences: the medicine, administered too eagerly, becomes a poison.

Consider the two paths that emerged from the apexanalytix data. On one side were the “Early Sprinters,” like Hilbert’s bank. They moved quickly, procuring and deploying new software and hardware marketed as quantum-resistant. They integrated these tools into their sprawling supplier platforms—the complex portals where thousands of vendors submit invoices, update their information, and manage contracts. On the other side were the “Cautious Optimizers.” These organizations took a different tack. They invested not in deployment, but in deep audit and simplification. They mapped their staggering digital ecosystems, asking a basic but brutal question: *What are we actually trying to protect, and what does it currently depend on?*

The difference in outcomes was startling. The Early Sprinters often found themselves with a patchwork. They had bolted new, advanced cryptographic modules onto old, creaking systems. The result was increased complexity—more code, more integration points, more potential for human error in configuration. One company in the study inadvertently created a security gap where its new quantum-safe supplier portal couldn’t communicate properly with its legacy internal payment system, requiring a temporary and risky “bridge” solution that auditors flagged as a critical weakness. Their platform was now a hybrid creature, part future, part past, and full of unforeseen seams.

The Cautious Optimizers, meanwhile, discovered something profound in their audit. They found that their biggest vulnerability wasn’t the future quantum attack. It was the present-day chaos. Many were maintaining dozens, sometimes hundreds, of different cryptographic standards across their supplier networks, a tangled legacy of years of mergers, acquisitions, and IT projects. By focusing on simplifying and standardizing their *current* security posture, they eliminated a vast landscape of *existing* threats—outdated encryption, weak password policies, unmonitored vendor access points. They were fortifying the castle they actually lived in, not just building a fancy new gate for a besieging army that might arrive in a decade.

So, what’s happening here? The apexanalytix researchers point to a concept well-known in systems engineering but often forgotten in tech hype cycles: **the maturity curve.**[^1] Genuinely transformative technologies don’t arrive perfectly formed. They evolve through stages of instability, standardization, and finally, robustness. The cryptographic algorithms designed to withstand quantum computers are in the earliest, most volatile phase. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) is still in the process of finalizing its official post-quantum cryptographic standards, a years-long global competition to vet the most promising candidates. What is sold as “quantum-safe” today might be a deprecated, compromised algorithm tomorrow. By locking it into critical infrastructure now, you may be painting a target on your back.

This presents a classic Gladwellian puzzle. Imagine you’re the CEO of a manufacturing giant with 10,000 suppliers. You have a limited security budget. Do you pour it into experimental, next-generation crypto to guard against a futuristic threat? Or do you use it to ruthlessly consolidate and monitor the access points you have right now, knowing that most cyberattacks exploit known, unpatched weaknesses? The apexanalytix data suggests the latter is not just prudent—it’s the strategically superior way to *eventually* become quantum-ready. A simple, well-understood, and meticulously managed system is far easier to upgrade wholesale when the time is right than a complex, fragmented one.

This brings us back to Mark Hilbert. When I spoke to him recently, he reflected on his bank’s journey. The initial push to future-proof was driven by fear of falling behind. But the project, he admits, became a “forced discovery” of their own IT sprawl. They ended up pausing the wholesale deployment of new quantum tools. Instead, they used the momentum and funding to launch a massive, two-year “cryptographic inventory” across their entire supplier network. It was tedious, unglamorous work. But by the end, they had a clear map and a radically simplified system. “Paradoxically,” Hilbert told me, “trying to get ready for tomorrow forced us to finally understand today. And understanding today is the only real foundation for any tomorrow.”

The lesson here isn’t to ignore quantum computing. The threat is real. The lesson is about the *sequence* of wisdom. In a world obsessed with the next leap, the most powerful move is often a step back. It is the audit before the upgrade, the simplification before the sophistication. The future doesn’t belong to those who buy the shiniest new lock first. It belongs to those who first take the time to understand what they’re really trying to lock up, and why the old locks were so hard to manage in the first place. Sometimes, the best way to run ahead is to walk slowly, and look down.

[^1]: This curve is famously illustrated in the Gartner Hype Cycle, but the underlying principle is observed across fields from aviation to medicine. New solutions create new problems before they mature. The early adoption of airplane cockpit automation, for instance, led to a new type of accident where pilots, bored and disengaged, failed to intervene when the automaton failed. The solution had to mature to include better human-machine interaction.
