SUBJECT LINE: The Regulation That Made Markets Less Efficient

In early 2018, a fund manager named James Clunie sat at his desk in London and looked at his inbox with a mixture of curiosity and dread. For years, his morning had begun with a cascade of research emails from sell-side analysts—detailed reports on companies he might invest in, all delivered free of charge. But on January 3rd, the first trading day after MiFID II took effect, the emails stopped. Not gradually. All at once. Clunie later described the shift as “a vacuum, where once there was a flow.” He was not alone. Across Europe, portfolio managers watched the same thing happen. And in the months that followed, a quiet but profound transformation took place in the world of investing.

Which brings us to a puzzle. The regulation that caused this—the second Markets in Financial Instruments Directive, known as MiFID II—was designed to do something admirable: make the financial system more transparent. For decades, banks had bundled the cost of research with the cost of executing trades. An asset manager would pay a commission to a bank for trading stocks, and the bank would use a portion of that commission to provide research reports. The problem was that this created a conflict of interest—the manager might trade more often than was wise just to generate commissions to pay for research. And the costs were buried, invisible to the end investor. MiFID II’s solution was to unbundle: force asset managers to pay for research separately, and to account for it explicitly.

It sounds like a good idea. But here is the strange thing: the regulation that was supposed to make markets fairer ended up making them *less* efficient. How did that happen?

To understand, we need to go back to the mid-2000s, when the European Commission began designing the rule. The chief architect of MiFID II was a group of regulators and market experts, among them Fabrice Demarigny, then secretary general of the European Securities and Markets Authority’s predecessor. Demarigny and his team were focused on a real problem: the hidden costs of bundled research. They wanted to protect smaller investors from being ripped off by opaque fee structures.
