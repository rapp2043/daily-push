SUBJECT LINE: The Hidden Side Effect of Fixing Drug Prices

Imagine you’re a pharmacist in a small town in Maryland. It’s a Tuesday morning, and a regular customer—an elderly man managing his diabetes—is at the counter. He’s holding a prescription for a new, more effective insulin. Last month, he couldn’t afford it. But today, thanks to the state’s new Prescription Drug Affordability Board, or PDAB, the price has been capped. He gets his medicine. You ring him up, and you both share a quiet moment of relief. This is the story we want to believe. A simple, heroic narrative where a policy targets high prices and wins. And in that moment, for that man, it’s true.

But here’s the problem with that theory. It turns out that for every story of immediate relief, there is a shadow story, one of unintended consequences that unfold quietly, in boardrooms and supply chains, far from the pharmacy counter. The push for PDABs—now established in states like Maryland, Colorado, and Washington—is driven by a powerful and correct impulse: drug prices in America are often indefensible. Yet, as researcher James C. Robinson noted in *Health Affairs*, the fervor to act is running ahead of the evidence on *how* to act. We are prescribing a strong medicine without fully understanding its side effects.

So let’s consider the other case. Let’s talk about a different kind of board meeting. This one isn’t in a state capitol, but in the headquarters of a small biotech company. Their team of two dozen scientists has spent a decade and nearly all their venture capital developing a promising, highly specialized treatment for a rare form of cancer. It’s not a blockbuster drug; the patient population is tiny, a few thousand people nationwide. Their business plan hinges on a price that reflects that enormous risk and cost. Then, they see the model. A state PDAB, using a common evaluation method, is setting an “affordable” price benchmark based on the average cost of existing, less effective treatments. For this small company, that benchmark is a death sentence. The projected revenue no longer justifies continued production. The board faces a brutal choice: abandon the drug for that state’s patients, or operate at a loss and risk the entire company. What would you do?

This is the central tension. PDABs are designed to tackle the outrageous pricing of mass-market drugs, the ones that appear on TV ads. Their tools, like upper payment limits, are blunt instruments. But the pharmaceutical ecosystem isn’t monolithic. It contains both the predatory pricing of old drugs and the precarious economics of developing new ones for rare diseases. A policy aimed at the former can inadvertently crush the latter. As Robinson points out, one of the great unanswered questions is how these boards will define which drugs are “unaffordable” in the first place. Will it be every high-price drug? Or only those where the price isn’t justified by the clinical benefit? That distinction is everything, and most states are still figuring it out.[^1]

Then there’s the domino effect. Think of a drug manufacturer facing a price cap in Colorado. They can’t simply absorb that cost for one state; their pricing is national. So, they have a few options, none of them good for the policy’s goal. They could withdraw the drug from the Colorado market altogether, leaving those patients worse off. They could raise the price in other, non-capped states to make up the revenue—shifting the burden, not reducing it. Or, in a perverse twist, they could launch the drug at an even *higher* initial “list” price, anticipating the future caps and building a buffer. The result? The policy designed to lower prices could, in some scenarios, cause them to spike elsewhere or vanish entirely from local shelves.

We think of affordability as a simple math problem: high number bad, lower number good. But in complex systems, the solution to one math problem creates three new ones. A PDAB’s success isn’t measured just at the pharmacy register. It’s measured in the research labs that decide which diseases are worth pursuing, in the investment memos of venture capitalists, and in the access that patients in one state have versus another. The intention is to create fairness. The risk is creating a new kind of lottery, where your access to medicine depends on your governor’s regulatory strategy and a company’s national profit-and-loss calculus.

Back in that Maryland pharmacy, the story feels complete. The price was capped, the patient was helped. But the full story is still being written. It’s being written by analysts trying to predict manufacturer retaliation, by lawyers preparing for inevitable lawsuits, and by small biotech firms running the numbers in despair. The moral of the story isn’t that tackling drug prices is wrong. It’s that the easiest policy to applaud is often the hardest one to execute. We reached for a scalpel to perform precise surgery. We may find we are swinging a very heavy hammer in a room full of glass.

[^1]: This evaluation often borrows from frameworks like the one used by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER), a nonprofit that assesses drug value. ICER’s models are influential but also controversial, often criticized by innovators for underestimating the value of treatments for severe or rare conditions. State PDABs adopting such models wholesale inherit both their strengths and their contentious blind spots.
[^2]: There’s a precedent for this kind of withdrawal. When the Trump administration moved to force drug companies to include list prices in TV ads, several major pharmaceutical giants simply threatened to pull their advertising altogether. The rule was tied up in court and never implemented. It serves as a reminder that manufacturers have a powerful playbook for resisting price regulation.
[^3]: An often-overlooked complexity is the interaction with federal programs like Medicaid or the 340B drug discount program. A state price cap could accidentally lower the “ceiling” for Medicaid rebates or alter 340B pricing models, creating a financial shockwave for the safety-net hospitals that rely on those funds. The unintended consequences ripple far beyond the cash-paying customer.
