SUBJECT LINE: The One Thing That Makes Judges Ignore Your Apology

In a university laboratory not far from Chicago, a group of legal professionals—judges, prosecutors, and public defenders—sat down to review a series of cases. Each case file included a defendant's statement, delivered at sentencing. In one, a man convicted of embezzlement said, “I am deeply sorry for what I did. I let my family down, and I let my company down. I will spend the rest of my life trying to make it right.” In another, a different defendant offered a similar apology, almost word for word. But there was a twist: the researchers had randomly assigned each defendant’s photograph to the legal professionals. Some saw a photograph of the defendant with a clean-cut appearance; others saw a version with a more stereotypically “criminal” look. 

What do you think happened next? Take a guess. Would the apology help the defendant who looked like an upstanding citizen more than the one who looked like a threat? If you guessed yes, you’re not alone—but you’re also part of a troubling pattern that the researchers call “remorse bias.”

According to the new study, published in a peer-reviewed journal and reported by Phys.org, the legal professionals who read those cases consistently rated the remorse of the clean-cut defendants as more sincere. They were more likely to recommend a lenient sentence for those defendants. For the defendants whose photographs triggered a more negative first impression, the same apologies were judged as less genuine—and the recommended sentences were harsher. The gap was not small. It was, the researchers reported, statistically significant and consistent across multiple experiments.

But here’s the problem. The researchers didn’t just find that first impressions matter. They found something far more unsettling: the legal professionals were not aware of the bias. When asked afterward, most insisted that they had evaluated the apologies objectively. They pointed to specific words the defendant used, the emotional tone, the apparent sincerity. They believed they were being fair. Yet their judgments were systematically influenced by a single, irrelevant variable: the defendant’s appearance.

How does that happen? It turns out that our brains process remorse in a way that feels objective but is actually shaped by preexisting expectations. Psychologists call this “confirmation bias for emotional cues.” When we already have a negative impression of someone, we interpret their expression of remorse as performative—forced, insincere, strategic. When we have a positive impression, we see the same expression as authentic remorse. The emotional signal itself hasn’t changed. Our interpretation of it has.

This phenomenon echoes a famous line of research by the psychologist Emily Pronin, who found that people can detect bias in others but not in themselves. We all think we are the exception. The prosecutor honestly believes that his judgment of the defendant’s tears is pure; the judge is certain that her reading of the apology is unaffected by anything beyond the words. Yet the data from the new study suggest otherwise. The remorse bias is not a case of bad people making bad decisions. It is a case of ordinary people, doing their jobs as best they can, being led astray by the very machinery of human perception.

Think about what this means for the courtroom. Remorse is one of the most powerful factors in sentencing decisions. Judges and juries are trained to look for it. In many legal systems, a defendant who shows remorse can receive a significantly lighter sentence. But if the assessment of remorse is systematically biased against defendants who already look “dangerous” or “criminal,” then the very mechanism designed to humanize the process may be deepening inequality. Those who need the leniency most—because they are already judged harshly by stereotypes—are the least likely to be seen as genuinely sorry.[^1]

Now return to that university laboratory. The researcher who conducted the study, speaking to a reporter, offered a sobering conclusion: we cannot simply tell legal professionals to “be more objective.” The bias operates below conscious awareness. Instead, we may need structural reforms—such as removing photographs from sentencing files, or training decision-makers to consciously separate judgments of remorse from judgments of character.

The defendant who broke down in apology—the one whose photograph triggered a negative first impression—never stood a fair chance. His remorse was true, but it was not believed. And that, in the end, is the quiet injustice of the remorse bias: it does not punish the guilty. It punishes the simply unlucky.

[^1]: There is a long history of remorse in Western law, dating back to the medieval concept of “penitence” as a sign of spiritual reform. The idea that a sincere apology should mitigate punishment is deeply intuitive. But as this research shows, our intuitions about sincerity are themselves shaped by prejudice. What feels like fairness may be the subtlest form of bias.
