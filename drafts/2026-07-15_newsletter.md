SUBJECT LINE: The Hidden Rule of Tuberculosis Growth

In a small laboratory at the University of Michigan, a researcher stared at a time-lapse video. On the screen, individual *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* cells elongated, split, and started again. Something strange caught their attention. For generations, microbiologists had assumed that bacteria grow at variable rates: young cells, small and hungry, accelerate; older cells, burdened by damage, decelerate before division. That’s how it works for *E. coli*, for yeast, for almost every single-celled organism. But these TB cells seemed to follow a different rhythm altogether. They grew at the same pace from the moment they were born until the moment they split. It was as if they were running on a constant, unchanging clock.

Ask yourself: if you were a bacterium, would you grow faster when you are young or when you are old? Most people, including scientists, would guess that growth varies over a cell’s life. It makes intuitive sense. Young cells have more resources, less damage, fewer constraints. They should sprint. Older cells, preparing to divide, should jog. This is the textbook model, taught in every introductory biology course—the assumption that growth is a series of sprints and jogs, not a steady marathon. But what if that assumption was wrong?

A recent study, published this year in *Nature Microbiology*, has turned that assumption on its head, at least for one of the world’s deadliest pathogens. The researchers used microfluidic devices to trap individual TB bacteria and watched them continuously for days. Using high-resolution time-lapse microscopy, they measured each cell’s length every few minutes. What they saw was startling: every cell, regardless of its age, added length at a constant rate. A newborn cell and a cell about to split grew at exactly the same speed. There was no deceleration, no acceleration. Growth was, in the words of the researchers, “remarkably constant.”[^1]

Now, how could generations of scientists have missed this? The answer lies in the methods we used to measure bacterial growth. For decades, experiments were done in flasks or cultures of millions of cells. When you measure the average growth of a population, individual variations get smoothed out. It looks like the overall growth rate changes over time, because some cells are dividing and others are not. But when you zoom in to the single cell, the truth emerges: each cell maintains a steady pace from beginning to end. It turns out that the variability we thought existed was an artifact of looking at the crowd instead of the individuals.

But here’s the problem this discovery raises: if TB bacteria grow at a constant rate, what does that mean for treatment? Tuberculosis kills over a million people every year, and the standard antibiotic regimen takes six long months. One reason treatment is so prolonged is that we believed some TB cells slow down or go dormant, becoming tolerant to drugs. The new finding suggests that growth rate itself is not the source of that variation. A cell that survives antibiotics is not a cell that has “slowed its growth”; it must be using a different survival strategy altogether. That changes how we think about designing new drugs.[^2]

Think of it this way: suppose you are trying to hit a moving target. If the target speeds up and slows down unpredictably, you have to constantly adjust your aim. But if the target moves at a steady pace, you can line up your shot and fire with confidence. That is what this discovery does for drug design—it simplifies the target. It tells us that if a drug works against growth machinery, it should work equally well against all TB cells, regardless of their age. The focus should shift to understanding the *other* mechanisms that allow cells to survive, not on growth rate variation.[
