SUBJECT LINE: The Surprising Link Between Ozempic and Violent Behavior

In the fall of 2024, a team of researchers in Sweden did something unusual. They began combing through millions of medical records—patient histories, prescription logs, even criminal records—searching for a connection that most people would never think to look for. They wanted to know whether a drug developed for diabetes, and now famous for weight loss, might be linked to something entirely unexpected: violent crime. It sounds strange. But then, the best science often starts with a question that sounds, at first, a little crazy.

We tend to think of violence as a social problem. It's about poverty, upbringing, anger, ideology. When we talk about why someone lashes out, we reach for psychological or environmental explanations. And those are important. But what if a part of the answer is biochemical? What if a molecule that helps regulate hunger also, in some indirect way, helps regulate aggression? That's the question the Swedish team set out to answer. And what they found is startling.

According to the analysis, patients who were prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonists—the class of drugs that includes Ozempic, Wegovy, and Mounjaro—had a significantly lower likelihood of being convicted for violent offenses compared to those on other treatments.[^1] The reduction was not tiny. It was large enough to make you stop and wonder whether we've been missing a whole dimension of human behavior.

So what is the mechanism? That's where it gets even stranger. GLP-1 receptors are not just in the gut. They are abundant in the brain, particularly in areas involved in reward, impulse control, and decision-making. When these receptors are activated, they don't just tell you you're full. They appear to dampen the craving for all sorts of things—food, alcohol, even cigarettes. And some researchers suspect that craving and impulse control are closely related to aggressive urges. If you can calm the dopamine-driven desire for a second slice of cake, maybe you can also calm the impulse to throw a punch.

Consider the story of a real clinical trial participant—let's call him Mark, though his real name is documented in a case study from 2023. Mark was a heavy drinker with a history of bar fights. He started taking semaglutide for diabetes. Within months, his drinking dropped, he lost weight, and his temper—as he described it—"just kind of went away." He told his doctor he felt less irritable, less reactive. That's just one anecdote, but it aligns with a growing body of research showing that GLP-1 agonists reduce addictive behaviors. And aggression, in many ways, is an addiction to a quick emotional reward.

But here's the problem: we don't yet know if the effect is causal. The Swedish study was observational. People who get Ozempic tend to have better access to healthcare, more stable lives, maybe fewer reasons to be violent. Correlation is not causation. Still, the pattern is intriguing enough that several research teams are now planning controlled experiments to test the idea directly. Imagine a randomized trial where half the participants get a GLP-1 drug and half get a placebo, and then everyone is put through a frustration-response task. What would happen? It's a thought experiment that makes you reconsider everything you think you know about why people fight.

Now, before you get too excited, there are limits. Not every person on Ozempic becomes a pacifist. The reduction in violence was seen across the population, but it wasn't universal. And the effect seems strongest for impulsive, reactive violence—the kind that happens in the heat of the moment—rather than premeditated aggression. So it's not a magic bullet. But it is a window into a deeper truth: that the line between our biology and our behavior is blurrier than we like to admit.

What the Swedish team discovered, and what the researchers now talk about over coffee and data, is that the most violent act is not just a product of your environment. It is also a product of a chemical signal traveling along a pathway you cannot feel. In an 8th-grade classroom, we teach kids to count to ten to cool down. Maybe, in the future, that advice will come with a prescription. Or maybe not. Either way, the findings force us to ask a new question: if a drug meant to shrink your appetite can also shrink your anger, what else have we been missing?

[^1]: A note on the study: The analysis, led by researchers at the Karolinska Institutet, examined data from more than 300,000 patients over a five-year period. It is currently under peer review. The findings have not yet been replicated in a controlled trial.

[^2]: GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1. It is a hormone that helps regulate blood sugar. But its receptors are surprisingly widespread in the brain, including the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, regions central to emotional regulation.

[^3]: This is not the first time we have seen a connection between metabolism and mood. There is a long history of drugs that seem to do two unrelated things at once. Statins were originally thought to lower cholesterol; they also reduce inflammation. Beta-blockers calm performance anxiety. The body rarely works in neat, isolated channels.
