**SUBJECT LINE: Why Warning Labels Make Lies More Believable**

In the summer of 2023, a team of researchers led by Ziv Epstein at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology posed a simple question to a group of online participants.[^1] They showed people a series of news headlines. Some were true, some were false. For some of the false ones, they attached a clear, straightforward label: *This was generated by AI*. The question was basic: would the label, a tool of modern transparency, help people spot the lie? Common sense screams yes, of course. We are inundated with warnings about deepfakes and AI-generated propaganda. A label is a beacon, a signal to be skeptical. It’s a logical, almost elegant fix to a 21st-century problem. But when Epstein and his colleagues tallied the results, logic went out the window. The labels didn’t help. In fact, they did the opposite. When a false headline was tagged as AI-made, participants were *more* likely to believe it was true.

But here’s the problem with that theory. It feels backward. A warning is supposed to trigger caution, not credulity. We build systems of labeling—*Contents May Be Hot*, *Fact-Checked*—precisely to short-circuit deception. We trust the label to do the hard work of vigilance for us. So what happened in that digital lab? To understand why a caution sign can become a seal of approval, you need to look past the algorithms. You need to look back, more than a century, to an era of unregulated tonics and tainted meat, to the moment America first decided that a label could be a shield.

The story begins not with a scientist, but with a muckraker. In 1905, Samuel Hopkins Adams, a journalist for *Collier’s* magazine, was investigating the patent medicine industry. He walked into drugstores and saw shelves lined with bottles of “Dr. King’s New Discovery for Consumption” or “Mrs. Winslow’s Soothing Syrup.” They promised to cure everything from cancer to colic. They did not promise, as Adams discovered, that they were often just alcohol, opium, or colored water. There were no ingredient lists, no warnings about addiction, no guarantees of safety. The public, desperate for relief, had to take the manufacturer’s word for it. Adams’s subsequent series, “The Great American Fraud,” was a sensation. It depicted an industry built not on healing, but on hollow, often dangerous, promises. The public outrage was immediate and fierce. People felt betrayed. They demanded protection.

And then something strange happened. The industry, facing existential threat, did not fight the call for regulation outright. Some of its more cunning players pivoted. They saw that a label—a government-mandated one—could be repurposed. The Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 finally forced companies to list certain narcotics on their labels. The goal was transparency, a way for consumers to see the opium in their “soothing syrup.” But for some manufacturers, the label became a badge. *See?* it seemed to say. *We are compliant. We are official. We have nothing to hide.* The label, conceived as a warning, began to subtly morph into a token of legitimacy. The other ingredients, the unproven claims, the sheer ineffectiveness of the tonic—all of it could now hide in the shadow of that one, government-sanctioned stamp. The label didn’t just inform; it conferred a kind of borrowed trust. The system, designed to provoke skepticism, inadvertently provided a cover for everything it didn’t explicitly forbid.

This is the hidden thread that connects the 1906 medicine bottle to the 2023 AI-generated headline. When we see a formal label, our cognitive machinery doesn’t always engage in deeper scrutiny. Sometimes, it disengages. The label becomes a cognitive shortcut. A product with a warning about one specific risk is subconsciously categorized as “regulated” or “officially monitored.” Our brain takes a partial transparency for a comprehensive guarantee.[^2] We outsource our vigilance to the label itself. The MIT experiment reveals this paradox in a digital context. The “AI-generated” tag is a specific, technical warning. But to a brain conditioned by a century of regulatory labels, it can subtly signal that *someone is minding the store*. This content has been seen, categorized, and flagged by the platform. It has entered a system of oversight. The very act of labeling can falsely imply a level of review that makes the underlying content seem, perversely, more legitimate than unlabeled content floating in the wild.

So what are we to make of this? We tried to solve a problem of trust with a tool that can erode it in a different way. It’s a pattern that repeats whenever we try to automate discernment. Think of the “USDA Organic” seal. For many, it’s a shorthand for “healthy” and “ethical,” yet the complex regulations behind it allow for industrial-scale organic farming that may not match the consumer’s pastoral ideal. The label simplifies a messy reality, and in that simplification, trust is transferred, sometimes blindly.

Here’s a question for you. You’re scrolling through your feed. You see two headlines making an odd scientific claim. One has no notation. The other has a small, gray icon that says “AI-Assisted.” Which one are you *more* likely to pause and double-check? Our instinct, after learning about the MIT study, might be to point to the unlabeled one. But our deeper, older instinct—the one formed by trusting “FDA Approved” on a bottle—might quietly nudge us toward trusting the labeled one. It has been processed. It has been seen. The machine, in its act of flagging, has paradoxically humanized it.

Ziv Epstein’s research points to this exact cognitive trap. The label doesn’t activate our critical faculties; it can placate them. It turns a complex question of truth and falsehood into a simple binary: labeled or unlabeled. And in that trade, we can be fooled. The patent medicine hucksters of 1906 might have been shocked by deepfakes, but they would have instantly understood the principle: a well-placed mark of official attention, even a critical one, can become the very thing that disarms suspicion. We wanted a red flag. We accidentally built a green light.

[^1]: Epstein’s work is part of a broader examination of “algorithmic attenuation” – the way platform interventions can have weak or even reverse effects. Other researchers, like Stanford’s Jeff Hancock, have found similar backfire effects with some types of fact-checking labels, where the act of rating a claim can inadvertently reinforce its familiarity and thus its perceived plausibility among certain audiences.

[^2]: Psychologists call this “processing fluency.” Information that feels easier to process—because it’s familiar, simple, or, in this case, neatly categorized—is often judged as more true. The AI label provides a quick, easy explanation for why a headline might seem odd (“Oh, it’s AI-made”), which satisfies our need for an answer and reduces the mental effort needed to evaluate the claim’s actual truth. The label doesn’t prompt more thought; it brings thought to a premature close.
