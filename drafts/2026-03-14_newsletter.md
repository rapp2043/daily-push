SUBJECT LINE: How Paying for Grocery Bags Can Make Us Less Green

Garrett Hardin stood before a gathering of scientists in Washington D.C. in the late summer of 1968, ready to deliver a lecture that would etch his name into the foundation of modern environmental policy. He was not an ecologist by training, but a human ecologist—a professor of biology at the University of California, Santa Barbara, who spent his career worrying about people. On that day, he presented a deceptively simple parable. Picture a pasture, he urged his audience, open to all. Each herdsman, acting rationally in his own interest, will keep adding more cattle to graze. The benefit of each new animal goes entirely to the herdsman; the cost of overgrazing is shared by all. So, the incentive is always to add one more head of cattle, and then one more, until the common pasture is ruined for everyone. He called this relentless logic “The Tragedy of the Commons.” The solution, Hardin argued with grim finality, was not to appeal to conscience. Conscience, he wrote, only selects for self-elimination. The only way out was “mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon.” In other words, we needed rules. We needed laws. We needed policy. It was a compelling, airtight argument that would go on to justify decades of environmental regulation, carbon taxes, and cap-and-trade systems. The central premise was clear: to save the commons, you must replace fuzzy, unreliable values with hard, reliable incentives.

But here’s the problem with that theory. It turns out the policy cure might be poisoning the very well it draws from.

Consider a simple, modern commandment: the plastic bag fee. Over the last two decades, dozens of cities and countries have adopted small charges for single-use bags at checkout. The logic is pure Hardin. The bag is a tiny tragedy of the commons—its convenience is personal, its environmental cost is shared. A small financial disincentive should nudge us toward reusable totes. And by the numbers, it works spectacularly. In England, a 5-pence charge introduced in 2015 led to a 95% drop in plastic bag use at major supermarkets. A similar fee in Washington D.C. cut bag distribution in half within a year. The policy is a triumph. Case closed.

But then something strange happened. Researchers began to ask a different question. What if the transaction changes more than just our shopping habits? What if it changes our *motives*? A team of psychologists and behavioral scientists set out to investigate this subtle shift. They ran a series of experiments and surveyed existing policy outcomes, looking not at bag counts, but at mindsets. Their findings, detailed in a 2025 article for the Global Environmental Psychology Lab, were startling. They found evidence of a perverse side effect. When a green behavior—like bringing your own bag—becomes something you do to *avoid a fee*, it can gradually cease to be something you do *because you care about the planet*. The external incentive, however small, can “crowd out” the internal value. The financial signal, in essence, overwrites the moral one. The policy succeeds in changing the immediate action but risks eroding the foundational reason for the action in the long run. You stop seeing yourself as an environmentalist making a choice. You start seeing yourself as a consumer avoiding a surcharge.[^1]

This is more than a philosophical quibble. It points to a deep, recurring flaw in our collective reasoning. We have assumed, as Garrett Hardin did, that values are weak and incentives are strong. That we must build policy *despite* human nature. But what if that framework gets human nature backward?

To understand this, we need to leave the grocery line and visit a group of Israeli daycare centers in the late 1990s. Economists Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini conducted a now-famous experiment. Frustrated by parents picking up their children late, some centers introduced a small fine for tardiness. The result was not less lateness, but *more*. Before the fine, parents and teachers had a social contract underpinned by guilt and consideration. The fine, however small, transformed lateness from a moral breach into a priced service. It became something you could pay for, and many parents decided it was worth the price. The incentive didn’t reinforce the desired behavior; it replaced the moral reasoning that had been sustaining it. The researchers called this a “crowding-out” effect. The financial incentive had crowded out the parents’ sense of civic duty.

Now, transpose that lesson onto the grand scale of climate policy. Bruno Frey, a Swiss economist who has long studied the psychology of motivation, argues that this effect lurks everywhere. A tax break for installing solar panels might spur installations, but it can also redefine the act in the public mind from a collective moral good to a private financial calculation. A cap-and-trade system might efficiently reduce emissions, but by creating a market for pollution permits, it can subtly legitimize the act of polluting, framing it as just another business cost rather than a societal harm. The policy works on a spreadsheet, even as it might hollow out the communal ethic needed for the harder, unpaid sacrifices ahead. This is the paradox at the heart of the modern environmental movement: the very tools we’ve engineered to save the commons may be weakening our innate feeling that the commons is worth saving on its own terms.

So what are we to make of this? Do we abandon carbon taxes and bag fees? That would be a different kind of tragedy. The research isn’t saying the policies are bad; it’s saying they are incomplete. They are brilliant at solving the *calculus* of the problem—the herdsman’s math—but they are dangerously silent on the *story* we tell ourselves about who we are.

Perhaps the hardest lesson is that values are not a fragile, pre-modern relic to be swept aside by sleek policy. They are a renewable resource that requires cultivation. A fee can stop a behavior, but only a shared ethic can sustain it and grow it into something more. The most effective systems might be those that do both: that use the stick of incentive while continuously speaking the language of common cause, reminding us that the fee is not the *point*, but a regrettable tool to protect something priceless. It’s the difference between a society that doesn’t litter because there’s a fine, and one that doesn’t litter because it’s *unthinkable*.

Garrett Hardin was right about the mechanics of the pasture. Each herdsman, acting alone, will inevitably add that one extra cow. But he may have been wrong about the human heart inside the herdsman. The tragedy isn’t that our values are too weak to solve the problem. The tragedy would be if, in our rush to solve the problem with policy, we forgot to nourish those values at all. The ultimate commons we risk overgrazing is not the physical one, but the moral one. And no fee on earth can rebuild that.

[^1]: This crowding-out effect is notoriously difficult to measure at a societal scale, which is why the 2025 research is so intriguing. It attempts to move beyond laboratory experiments to study the "moral residue" of real-world policies. The researchers suggest the effect may be strongest when the incentive is perceived as controlling rather than supportive—when it feels like a stick, not a carrot for good behavior.
